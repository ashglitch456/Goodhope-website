# Retread Buffings: What an In-House Line Does With the Rubber It Removes

Published 2026-10-29. Pillar C.




Every retread starts by removing the old tread. That rubber does not disappear: it comes off the casing as buffings, and a line that retreads every day produces them every day. Fleets planning an in-house shop tend to budget for equipment and compound, then discover the buffing stream late. This guide covers what buffings are, what is known about where they go, and how Ontario's tire rules mention them.
Sourcing note: we could read search-result extracts of the regulator's documents, not the full texts, and we found no current price or volume data for buffings. The post says so where it matters and does not state figures we could not verify.

## What buffings are
Ontario's Resource Productivity and Recovery Authority (RPRA) describes retreading as the process of replacing the tread on a used tire with new tread, and buffings as the material removed during that process (Compliance Bulletin No. 9, May 2023). Ontario's Tires Regulation, O. Reg. 225/18, defines a tire retreader as a person who replaces the tread on worn tires with new tread so the tire can serve its original purpose.

## How Ontario's tire rules mention buffings
The RPRA bulletin is written for tire producers, who have resource recovery targets under the regulation. In summary:

- A producer can count the full calculated weight of a retreaded tire toward its target, but only if the tire was previously used and collected in Ontario.
- Buffings can count toward a producer's target too, again only when they come from used tires that were used and collected in Ontario.
- RPRA treats retreaders, haulers and processors as service providers, and retreaders register in RPRA's Registry.

What we could not confirm is how these rules apply to a fleet that retreads only its own casings. Whether your operation has to register, and what you must record about buffings, is a question for RPRA directly. For the wider regulatory picture, see our guide to Ontario's 2026 tire regulation amendments.

## Where buffings go
Trade articles describe buffings as a by-product of retreading that is usually sold or given to recyclers rather than landfilled. Reported end uses include crumb rubber, poured-in-place playground surfaces, bonded landscape surfaces and molded rubber products. The same trade coverage, which is more than a decade old, described periods of oversupply and weak prices for lower-quality material. We found no current figures, so do not plan on buffings as a revenue line. Treat them as a material you need a dependable outlet for.

## Planning for the buffing stream on your own line
Our reasoning, not a published figure:

- Capture it at the source. Buffing dust and chips are collected by the buffer's dust system. See our guide to dust collection and the combustible dust hazard, because fine rubber dust needs proper handling and housekeeping.
- Line up an outlet before you start. Ask a processor or recycler near you what condition they want the material in, whether they collect, and what they charge or pay. Get it in writing.
- Plan the storage. Decide where collected material sits, how much volume it makes at your throughput, and how often it leaves. A full bin that stops the buffer is a production problem.
- Keep records. Whatever RPRA requires of you, weights and destinations of buffings are worth logging, in the same way you keep a casing salvage rate.
- Include it in the cost model. A line's consumables plan should cover disposal as well as supply; see consumables supply planning.
