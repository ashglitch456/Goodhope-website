# Dual Tire Matching: Why Retread Diameter Control Matters on Your Own Line

Published 2026-10-30. Pillar A.

On a tractor drive axle or a trailer, two tires share one wheel position. If they are not the same height, the smaller one is dragged along the road by the larger one. Fleets that retread, and especially fleets that plan to retread in-house, have an extra reason to care: a retread's final diameter depends on the casing you started with and how you prepared it. This guide covers what trade sources say about dual matching, where they disagree, and what a retread line can reasonably measure and control.

Sourcing note: we could read search-result extracts of the articles below, not their full texts, and we could not open the TMC recommended-practice documents themselves. Where sources disagree we say so rather than pick a number.


## Why matching matters
Automotive Fleet's guidance on matching duals explains the mechanism: if the diameter or circumference of the two tires differs even slightly, the smaller tire scrubs along the pavement trying to make up the distance the larger one travels, and that scrubbing is what shortens tread life. FleetOwner calls diameter the biggest enemy of a dual assembly and notes that tire sizing is nominal, meaning approximate, so two tires with the same size marking are not automatically the same height.

The Retread Industry Association (retread.org) adds a retreading point: mismatched tires in dual positions cause many problems, not the least of which is that their retreadability can be impaired. Our inference: a casing worn unevenly by scrubbing is a weaker candidate for its next life. See our guide to underinflation and casing life for a related casing-wear mechanism.


## What limit do the sources give?
The published numbers are not uniform:

- FleetOwner ("Dual matching nightmares"): the maximum allowable difference in diameter between dual tires is 1/4 inch, measured as the difference in height with the tires standing side by side off the vehicle.
- Fleet Equipment ("Closing the retread gap for commercial trucks"): cites the Technology & Maintenance Council's radial tire service manual as allowing a diameter difference of no more than 1/4 inch for radial assemblies.
- A training study guide (open-exam-prep.com) cites 1/2 inch of diameter or 3/4 inch of circumference, attributing it to TMC Recommended Practice RP 217. We could not verify that attribution, and it is a study aid rather than the primary document. Advanced Fleet Management also gives a half-inch figure.
- Our arithmetic: circumference is pi times diameter, so a 1/4 inch difference in diameter is about 0.79 inch of circumference, roughly in line with the 3/4 inch circumference figure that some sources pair with 1/4 inch.

Because the sources disagree, treat any single number here as a starting point and check the current TMC recommended practice and your tire manufacturers' guidance. Whatever limit your fleet adopts, write it down and apply it the same way every time.


## Tread depth is not a substitute for measuring
Advanced Fleet Management notes that when two tires share the same size, make and tread design, a common matching rule is to keep tread depth within 4/32 inch of each other. It adds that retreads are the exception: two retreads can have the same outside diameter with different casings, so they should be measured directly rather than matched by label or by tread depth alone. The same source notes that, where the road has crown, the smaller-diameter tire is typically placed on the inside.


## What an in-house line can control
Our reasoning, not a published figure. A fleet running its own line sees every casing before and after the retread, which makes diameter something it can log rather than discover on the vehicle:

- Record the finished diameter or circumference. Measure each finished retread and keep the number with the casing record, in the same way you keep a casing salvage rate. A tape or a caliper-style gauge is all it takes; the discipline is the point.
- Control buffing consistency. The buffing step sets the surface the new tread is applied to. Consistent radius and depth are part of casing prep quality.
- Pair at the point of mounting. Sort finished retreads by measured size and tread design so that duals are matched when they leave your shop, not when a driver complains about wear.
- Do not mix constructions. The retread.org primer warns against mixing radial and bias-ply tires on one axle.
- Match tread design too. Our guide to tread design selection covers why one pattern does not fit every position.
