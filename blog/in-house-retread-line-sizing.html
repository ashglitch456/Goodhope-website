How Big Should an In-House Retread Line Be? What Industry Plant Sizes Tell Fleets

Retread plants range from about 20 tires a day to 1,000 or more. How to size an in-house retread line to your fleet's casing volume without guessing.

Published October 3, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

Most fleets that look at retreading for themselves get stuck on one question: how big does the operation have to be to make sense? The honest answer is that nobody publishes a universal break-even volume. What the industry does publish is how big real retread plants are, and that is a useful frame for sizing your own.

This guide uses those published figures, plus simple arithmetic you can run with your own numbers. It does not use an invented fleet example, because any invented result would only tell you about the assumptions behind it.

What the industry data says about plant size

  • Output ranges widely. Retread.org describes retread plants ranging from small operations producing about 20 retreaded tires a day to very large plants processing 1,000 or more a day.
  • Most plants are small businesses. The U.S. Tire Manufacturers Association (USTMA) fact sheet describes retreads as made primarily by small independent businesses that typically employ 10 to 60 workers.
  • The industry is concentrated in fewer plants. The same fact sheet puts U.S. retread facilities at about 500 in 2025, down from more than 3,000 in 1982, with about 15 million tires retreaded a year, including nearly 44% of commercial truck tires in the U.S. and Canada.
  • Published daily averages exist but are dated. Industry figures cited in trade press put the average medium and heavy truck retreader at 246 tires a day and light truck at 15.3, in data that describes the early 2010s. Use them as a rough sense of scale rather than a current benchmark.

The takeaway is that a viable retread operation can be small. Production at the low end of the published range is measured in tens of tires a day, not hundreds. That does not mean any fleet should build one, but it does mean the size of the plants that exist is not, by itself, a reason to rule it out.

Sizing from your own numbers, not an industry average

Plant averages describe retreaders who sell to many customers. A fleet retreading only its own casings is sizing to a different number: the casings it generates. A simple way to estimate that:

  • Step 1: Count annual casing removals. How many casings come off your trucks and trailers each year? Your tire records already hold this.
  • Step 2: Apply your own acceptance rate. Not every removed casing passes inspection. Your scrap data, or your current retreader's reject rate, gives you the share that does. We do not quote a typical figure here because we could not find a current, citable one.
  • Step 3: Turn it into a daily rate. Divide retreadable casings by your working days. As plain arithmetic, a line making 20 retreads a day, the low end of the published range, over 250 working days is 5,000 retreads a year. The 250 days is our assumption, so use your own calendar.
  • Step 4: Check the bottleneck. Output is set by the slowest step. For most lines that is the curing chamber, so chamber capacity and cure cycles decide how many tires a shift can finish. Our curing chamber guide covers the options.
  • Step 5: Decide on shifts. If your daily volume exceeds what one shift can finish, you either need more capacity or a second shift. Staffing then becomes the constraint. See our look at the technician shortage.

Where a smaller fleet may be better off not building

Retreading in-house is not right for every fleet. If your casing volume is low enough that the equipment would sit idle much of the time, or you cannot staff trained technicians, a good third-party retreader may serve you better. We would rather you reach that conclusion before buying than after. Our guides to what it costs to set up a retread shop and to in-house payback are written to help you test that honestly, and the ROI calculator lets you run your own numbers.

Starting small and scaling

A line does not have to be built at full size on day one. Capacity can follow demand if you choose equipment with that in mind. We supply electric curing chambers from 4-tire starter units up, so a fleet can start at a scale that matches its casing volume and add capacity as the operation proves itself. Whatever you start with, plan the consumables and spare components alongside the machines. Our guide to consumables supply planning covers that side.
