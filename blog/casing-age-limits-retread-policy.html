Casing Age Limits: How to Set a Retreadability Policy That Doesn't Scrap Good Casings

Is there a hard age limit on a retreadable truck tire casing? What industry panelists said, how fleet policies vary, and how to build an inspection-first casing policy.

Published October 3, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

Ask ten fleets how old a casing can be before it should be scrapped instead of retreaded and you will get several different answers. Some fleets set a hard age cutoff. Others set none and let inspection decide. The choice matters because every casing you scrap early is a retread you paid to give up.

This guide lays out what a published industry panel actually said, how that compares with typical fleet practice, and how to write a policy you control. That last part matters most if you are weighing running your own retread line, because a casing policy is a decision you would make yourself rather than inherit.

What an industry panel said about casing age

Modern Tire Dealer reported on a panel at the Commercial Tire, Retread & Recycling Conference on casing age and retread performance. The question put to the panelists was direct: is there an age limit on tires you will retread? According to that report:

  • Representatives of two major tire manufacturers said they have no age limit for retreading their tires. Their position was that age is not the issue and that casing integrity is judged through inspection.
  • One manufacturer was the exception. It recommended up to seven years for long-haul service and up to ten years for severe service and pickup-and-delivery work.
  • The panelists agreed that casings can last more than ten years based on the data available, with the application and proper inspection as the deciding factors.
  • One panelist put it as care and use being better indications of a casing's soundness than chronology.

So the industry does not have one answer. Published guidance ranges from "no age limit, inspect every casing" to "seven to ten years depending on duty cycle." Neither end of that range treats a casing's birth date as the whole story.

What fleet policies look like in practice

Fleet practice tends to be stricter than the panel positions above. Trade coverage has described some fleets setting limits as short as three or four years, while many sit at five or six. The same coverage describes a trend toward longer limits, with some fleets now at seven or even eight years. Treat those as reported ranges rather than a standard. Nobody publishes one, and practice varies by operation.

A tight age limit has a cost that is easy to miss. It shrinks the pool of casings eligible for retreading, which means more tires bought new that could have been retreaded. A cutoff that is too short can raise your tire spend instead of lowering it. That is an inference from how the policy works, not a measured figure, so test it against your own scrap data before changing a policy.

Why age alone is a weak filter

Two casings of the same age can be in very different condition. Heavy under-inflation, repeated overloading, impact damage and poorly done repairs all age a casing faster than the calendar does. A casing that ran at correct pressure on a regional route may be sounder than a younger one that was run flat. That is the logic behind the panel's emphasis on care and use. For the care side of that equation, see our guide to inflation and casing life.

It is also why inspection carries so much weight. A visual and tactile check catches the obvious problems, and non-destructive testing catches what the eye cannot. We cover both in the 5-point casing check and in our look at shearography and x-ray inspection.

Building a casing policy you can defend

A workable policy does not need to be complicated. These are the elements worth deciding in writing, whether you retread through a vendor or on your own line:

  • Application tiers. Long-haul and severe-service casings do not necessarily deserve the same age treatment. The one published manufacturer guideline above makes exactly that distinction.
  • An age trigger for closer inspection, not automatic scrap. Using age to decide how hard you look is easier to defend than using it to decide whether you look at all.
  • Damage criteria. List what scraps a casing outright (for example, certain structural damage) and what can be repaired.
  • Repair history. Record repairs so the next inspection knows what it is looking at.
  • Storage. Casings that sit outdoors or in poor conditions between service and retreading are a separate risk. See TIA's storage standards.
  • Tracking. Keep scrap-reason data so you can test whether your age rule is catching real defects or discarding sound casings.

Where an in-house line changes the picture

When a third party retreads your casings, the policy is shaped by what that plant will accept and what its warranty terms allow. When you run your own line, you set the acceptance criteria and you see the scrap data directly. The trade-off is that you carry the responsibility for the inspection standard. A shop that sets no age limit has to be able to back that decision with a rigorous inspection step.
