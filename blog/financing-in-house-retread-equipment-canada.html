Financing an In-House Retread Line in Canada: Cash, Equipment Loans, CSBFP and Leasing

How Canadian fleets can pay for retread equipment: what BDC, the Canada Small Business Financing Program and CRA's lease rules actually say, and how to match payments to the savings a line produces.

Published November 22, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

Equipment is usually the largest cheque a fleet writes when it brings retreading in-house. That makes how you pay for it almost as important as what you buy. The same buffer, builder and curing chamber can look like a fast payback or a slow one depending on whether you pay cash, borrow, or lease, and on how the payments line up with the savings the line produces.

This guide walks through the main ways Canadian fleets finance retread equipment, using terms published by the lenders and government programs themselves. It's general information to help you ask better questions of your bank and accountant, not financial or tax advice. Rates and program rules change, so confirm current terms before you commit.

Start with what you're financing against

Financing only makes sense when you compare it to the savings stream it pays for. The core of that stream is the price gap between a retread and a new tire. David Stevens, managing director of the Tire Retread & Repair Information Bureau (TRIB), has put typical savings at 30% to 50% of the cost of a newly manufactured tire (as quoted by Work Truck). In-house, your actual savings per retread depend on your volume, your casing salvage rate, and your consumables and labour costs, which we break down in Fleet Retreading ROI: When In-House Pays for Itself and Casing Salvage Rate.

Once you have a realistic monthly savings figure, the financing question gets simple: does the monthly payment sit comfortably below the monthly savings, from the month the line starts producing? If it does, the line can carry its own debt. If it doesn't, you need a longer term, a principal holiday at the start, more volume, or a smaller first phase.

Option 1: Pay cash

Paying outright means no interest and no lender paperwork. The cost is liquidity. An in-house line doesn't just need equipment. It also needs a starting stock of tread rubber, cushion gum, envelopes and repair materials, plus the working capital to carry casings through the process (see Consumables Supply Planning). A fleet that drains its cash buying equipment can end up short on the inventory that makes the equipment productive. Cash works best for fleets with a strong balance sheet, or for a modest first phase such as a repair bay.

Option 2: A term equipment loan

A conventional equipment loan from your bank or from the Business Development Bank of Canada (BDC) spreads the cost over years while you own the equipment from day one. BDC's published terms for its equipment loan include:

  • Financing of up to 125% of the equipment's purchase price, with the extra room intended for costs like shipping, installation and training.
  • Repayment terms of up to 12 years, with payments that can be matched to your cash-flow cycle.
  • Up to 24 months of postponed principal payments at the start of the loan.

The principal holiday matters for a retread line. A new line has a ramp-up period while crews train, casing supply builds and quality settles. Deferring principal through that window lets early savings build up before full payments start. BDC also notes a down payment may be required depending on the financing structure and your risk profile, so treat these as maximums, not guarantees.

Option 3: A government-backed CSBFP loan through your bank

The Canada Small Business Financing Program (CSBFP) doesn't lend directly. The federal government shares the risk with your bank or credit union, which can make lenders more willing to finance a newer venture like an in-house retread line. According to Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada (ISED):

  • The maximum per borrower is $1.15 million: up to $1 million in term loans and up to $150,000 in lines of credit.
  • Within the term-loan limit, up to $500,000 can go to equipment and leasehold improvements.
  • Since July 4, 2022, term loans for equipment and leasehold improvements can run up to 15 years.
  • There's a registration fee of 2% of the loan amount, which can be financed as part of the loan.
  • The maximum floating rate on a term loan is the lender's prime rate plus 3%, which includes a 1.25% annual administration fee. Lines of credit can be charged up to prime plus 5%.

The program is aimed at small businesses and has its own eligibility rules, including a revenue ceiling. Larger fleets may not qualify, so check ISED's current eligibility criteria with your lender. The leasehold-improvement allowance can matter as well, since many shops need electrical, air or ventilation work before the equipment arrives (see Workplace Safety for an In-House Retread Shop in Ontario and Buffing Dust Collection).

Option 4: Leasing

Leasing turns the purchase into a regular operating cost. Tax treatment is where leases differ most from loans, and it's worth understanding before you sign:

  • By default, lease payments are deductible. CRA's guidance says a business can deduct lease payments incurred in the year for property used in the business. In that case the lessor, not you, claims Capital Cost Allowance (CCA) on the equipment.
  • The section 16.1 election changes that. Under section 16.1 of the Income Tax Act, the lessor and lessee can jointly elect to treat the lease, for the lessee's tax purposes, as if the lessee bought the property with borrowed money. The lessee then deducts a notional interest portion and claims CCA instead of deducting the full payments. CRA indicates the election is filed with your return for the year the lease is entered into (Form T2145), and a CRA interpretation notes it can't be late-filed. Some property is excluded.

Which treatment comes out ahead depends on your tax position and on the CCA class your equipment falls into. We cover CCA classes, the accelerated write-offs, and the open questions about how retread equipment is classified in The Tax Case for Bringing Retreading In-House. Have your accountant compare the lease and the loan on an after-tax basis.

One practical point: read a lease's end-of-term terms closely. Buyout price, return conditions and renewal terms decide whether you end up owning a line you can keep running for years, or handing it back just as it's paid for.

How to compare offers fairly

  • Compare total cost, not the monthly payment. A longer term lowers the payment but raises the total interest paid.
  • Match the term to the equipment's working life. Retread machinery is built for long service. A term that ends well inside its working life gives you years of savings with no payments.
  • Count every fee. Registration fees, administration fees and lease documentation fees all belong in the comparison.
  • Line up the start date with production. A principal holiday or a staged draw that matches commissioning protects cash flow while the line ramps up.
  • Finance in phases if that fits. Many fleets start with a repair bay and casing program before adding a full line, which keeps the first financing request smaller (see Bringing Tire Work In-House in Stages).

Financing is part of the independence case

When a fleet relies on an outside retreader, the equipment cost is built into every retread it buys, and the fleet never builds an asset. When the fleet finances its own line, the payments end, and it's left owning the equipment, the process and the casing data. That's the core of the argument we make in The Real Cost of Franchise Lock-In vs. Running Your Own Retread Line. Whichever route you choose, the goal is the same: payments the line's own savings can cover, on a term that leaves you with years of debt-free production.

What lenders will ask for

Most lenders want a clear equipment quote, a description of what the equipment does, and a simple business case: volume, savings per retread, and when production starts.
