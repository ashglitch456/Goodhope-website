Final Inspection After Curing: The Last Quality Gate on a Retread Line

What a post-cure final inspection checks on a retreaded truck tire — tread alignment, cushion gum line, inner liner, repairs and sidewall — why it's done hot, and how to set it up on an in-house line.

Published November 17, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

Most quality talk in retreading happens at the front of the line: casing inspection, buffing, repairs. Those steps decide whether a casing is worth retreading. But one check decides whether a finished retread is fit to leave the building, and that's the final inspection after curing.

The Tire Retread & Repair Information Bureau (TRIB) describes it simply. Every retreaded tire goes through a final inspection, which ensures that only tires meeting industry quality standards leave the retread plant. A tire that passes is trimmed to remove excess rubber and painted. A tire that fails doesn't ship. This guide covers what that inspection looks at, why timing matters, and how to set it up on your own line.

Why the inspection happens while the tire is still hot

Fleet Equipment's guide to retreads makes a point that's easy to skip on a busy line: inspect the tire hot, straight out of the curing chamber. Flaws in the inner liner, the casing and any repairs show up clearly while the tire is still hot. Bauer Built's description of the retread process says the same: tires go to final inspection while still hot because anomalies are easier to spot.

In practice, that means the inspection station sits right next to the chamber. A finished tire shouldn't sit on a rack cooling while it waits for an inspector.

What a final inspection looks at

Trade descriptions of the process agree on the core areas. Fleet Equipment lists tread alignment and freedom from defects, plus the appearance of the sidewall, inner liner and cushion gum. Here's how that breaks down on a precure line:

1. The tread: alignment and defects

The precured tread should be centred on the casing and run true all the way round, with no visible defects in the tread rubber. A tread that has shifted during building or enveloping shows up here. So does damage from handling.

2. The cushion gum line: the envelope's report card

The bond line between tread and casing is where curing problems show first. Overdrive's explainer on retreading puts it plainly. The cushion gum should be smooth. If the curing envelope leaked, an inspector can usually see it right away, because the gum will be wrinkled or have bubbles in it.

That makes the gum line a direct check on your enveloping. A pattern of wrinkled or bubbled gum points to envelope vacuum integrity — worn envelopes, a poor seal, or a leaking valve stem — rather than the individual tire.

3. The inner liner and repairs

Curing puts the whole casing through heat and pressure, including any section or spot repairs made before building. Fleet Equipment's point about hot inspection applies most here: liner and repair flaws are clearest straight out of the chamber. An inspection spreader opens the beads so the inspector can see and light the whole liner.

4. The sidewall

The sidewall gets a final look for any damage, and for anything missed or caused since the initial inspection. It's also where the retread's identification goes. Fleet Equipment notes that once a tire meets all the standards, the retread DOT number is branded into the sidewall to complete the process. US retread identification rules are covered in DOT certification for an in-house line.

Check the cure record, not just the tire

A visual check can't see everything that happened inside the chamber, so the cure record is part of final inspection too. Fleet Equipment's tour of one major retread system's training centre describes a curing chamber that tracks temperature and pressure automatically and raises alarms if something goes wrong during the cycle.

Sequence matters as well as setpoints. A Goodyear retreading patent (US 5,151,148) describes why: if too much pressure is applied before the tread edges have sealed to the casing, problems follow. Its system applies envelope pressure only after the chamber has reached a set temperature and a set time has passed. Whatever chamber you run, the inspector should be able to confirm the tire's cycle ran to the specified profile without alarms. If it didn't, the tire is held for review whatever it looks like.

Setting it up on your own line

Published sources describe what to inspect. How you organise the inspection is up to you. Here's how we suggest an in-house shop structures it:

  • Put the station next to the chamber. That way every tire is inspected hot, as the sources recommend.
  • Use a written checklist. Cover tread alignment, gum line, liner and repairs, sidewall, and cure record. Have the inspector sign off on each tire.
  • Separate "reject" from "rework." Some findings, such as excess rubber, are trimming issues. Others, such as a bond-line defect, mean the tire doesn't ship. Your procedures should say which is which, following your tread and cushion supplier's guidance.
  • Log every finding and look for patterns. One bubbled gum line is a tire problem. Several in a week is an envelope or sealing problem. Final-inspection records are how you catch upstream issues early. They also feed directly into your shop KPIs.
  • Pair it with uniformity checks where needed. Visual inspection doesn't measure run-out. For that, see Radial Run-Out and Balance.

Why this matters more when you run the line

When a fleet sends casings out, final inspection is the retreader's job. When the fleet runs its own line, the inspection belongs to the fleet, and so does the result. A good final-inspection routine is how an in-house shop shows its retreads are as good as the casing and process allow. It also keeps the line's other quality checks honest.
