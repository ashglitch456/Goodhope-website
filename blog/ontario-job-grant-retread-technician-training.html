Paying for Retread Technician Training in Ontario: What the Ontario Job Grant Covers

Running your own retread line means building your own bench of skilled technicians. What Ontario's redesigned job grant pays for, who has to deliver the training, and what it won't cover.

Published November 15, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

When a fleet sends its casings to an outside retreader, the retreader supplies the skilled people. When the fleet runs its own line, it has to build that bench itself. We covered the labour market in our post on the tire technician shortage. This post covers one way an Ontario employer can share the cost of formal training: the province's job grant.

Sourcing note: we read the official guidelines and the program pages through search extracts, and some details came from third-party grant guides. We've marked those. Program rules change, so confirm the current guidelines with the Ministry or Employment Ontario before you plan around any figure here.

A redesigned program for 2026

The Canada-Ontario Job Grant has been replaced by the Ontario Job Grant. Ontario.ca now redirects the old program page to the new one, and the new application guidelines took effect May 1, 2026. Third-party grant guides report that the old program was paused in November 2025 for a ministry review before the relaunch.

What it pays

  • Up to $10,000 per trainee. According to the official guidelines, that's the maximum ministry contribution toward direct training costs.
  • Up to $15,000 per trainee in one case. The guidelines raise the cap for employers with fewer than 100 employees who are training new hires who were previously unemployed.
  • Cost-shared. The employer pays part of the cost. Third-party guides report that employers with 100 or more employees must pay at least half. We couldn't confirm the smaller-employer share from the official text, so check it directly.
  • Materials and travel are capped. The guidelines allow up to $500 per trainee for materials, textbooks and software. They also allow up to $500 for travel, but only where the trainee travels far enough to qualify.

Who has to deliver the training

  • A third-party provider. Ontario.ca says an approved third-party provider must deliver the training. Training delivered by your own staff doesn't qualify.
  • Eligible provider types. Third-party summaries of the 2026 guidelines list publicly assisted colleges and universities, registered career colleges, union-based training centres, sector councils and industry associations, among others. Reported conditions include a physical presence in Ontario and at least two years of offering the training.
  • Other conditions reported in the guidelines: training delivered in Ontario, only to the trainees named in the application, and finished within 52 weeks. Apprenticeship training is not eligible.

Training that fits a retread line

The Tire Industry Association's Commercial Tire Service (CTS) program is the usual benchmark for commercial tire work. TIA describes Basic CTS as a 200-level course for training new hires, and Certified CTS as a course for experienced employees with at least two years of hands-on work. The Ontario Tire Dealers Association has offered TIA training programs in Ontario.

Whether a particular course qualifies depends on the provider meeting the grant rules, so ask the provider and Employment Ontario before you count on funding. CTS covers commercial tire service broadly. It isn't a retread-process course, so plan for hands-on training on your own equipment as well.

What the grant won't pay for

  • Wages. Trainee wages aren't an eligible cost.
  • Anything you've already paid for. Costs incurred before your funding agreement takes effect aren't eligible. Apply before the training starts.
  • Training by your own people. In-house instruction doesn't count, because the provider has to be a third party.
  • Apprenticeships. These aren't eligible under the 2026 guidelines.

Two points from us: equipment commissioning and setup guidance from a machinery supplier, including ours, is part of buying the equipment, not grant-eligible training, and we won't claim otherwise. The grant also doesn't pay for equipment. For that side, see our posts on financing an in-house line and the tax treatment of retread equipment.

Why this matters for the in-house decision

This section is our own reasoning. Retreading's cost advantage over new tires is well documented. Our setup cost guide covers the figures, and training is one of the costs that isn't equipment. A fleet that relies on an outside retreader is paying for that retreader's skills on the retreader's schedule. A fleet that runs its own line keeps those skills in-house, along with control over pace, quality and its own casings. We make the wider case in franchise lock-in vs. running your own line.

A training grant doesn't change whether a line makes sense for your volume. It can lower the cost of getting your first technicians to a recognised standard, provided you apply before the training starts.
