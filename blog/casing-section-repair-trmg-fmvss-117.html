Section Repairs on Truck Casings: What TRMG's Recommended Practice and FMVSS 117 Say

A casing that clears inspection often still needs a repair. What the TRMG section-repair recommended practice and FMVSS 117 actually say about nail holes, cord exposure and bead-area limits, and what an in-house line should build into its repair step.

Published October 26, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

Most casings that reach a retread line are not flawless. A nail hole, a cut or a stone bruise is routine, and whether a casing is worth retreading often comes down to whether the damage can be repaired properly. Two sets of published rules shape that decision: the industry's recommended practices for tire repair, and the federal retread standard, FMVSS 117.

This guide summarizes what those documents say, based on the publicly available descriptions we could find. We were not able to read the full text of the recommended practice, so it does not reproduce its injury chart or repair dimensions. Those limits belong to the document itself, so use the current copy when you set shop procedures. For the repair step in context, see our guides to skiving casing damage before buffing and comparing skive repair tools.

What the TRMG recommended practice covers

The Tread Rubber and Tire Repair Materials Manufacturers' Group (TRMG) publishes recommended practices, and the Tire Retread & Repair Information Bureau (TRIB) lists them free to view on its website. Several relate directly to repair: one covers repair materials and cushion gums (RP-02-15), one covers tools for repairs (RP-02-12), and one covers off-the-road tire repair (RP-02-16).

The one aimed at truck casings is RP-02-17, titled "Section Repair of Radial Ply Truck Tires in a Full Service Repair Facility." The Tire Industry Association's copy is marked as updated in May 2020. Its published outline covers:

  • Inspection of the tire before repair, on a spreader and with adequate lighting.
  • Which repairs are permissible.
  • A section repair injury chart.
  • Repair unit selection.
  • Repair methods.
  • Finishing.

Two limits are described in the available summaries. The non-repairable bead area is measured from the bead toe, and no section repairs may be made in the bead areas where casing penetration or steel cord damage has occurred. TRMG's glossary also defines a section repair as one made when an injury has extended through 75% or more of the actual plies, or completely through the casing in the tread or sidewall areas. That definition matters because it separates a minor injury repair from a structural one, and the document applies different rules to each.

What FMVSS 117 requires

FMVSS 117 is the federal standard for retreaded pneumatic tires, and it limits what a retreader may do to a casing. As described in the current regulation text, a casing is off-limits if bead wire or cord fabric is already exposed before processing, and also if any cord fabric is exposed during processing, such as during buffing. The exceptions are fabric at a splice where ply segments overlap and belt-material fabric, and even those must not be penetrated or removed. A separate provision bars adding or removing belts or plies during processing. See our guide to DOT certification for an in-house retread line for the wider picture.

NHTSA's published interpretations of the standard add detail that matters at the repair bench:

  • Nail holes. A 1972 NHTSA interpretation says it does not treat nail holes as exposure of cord fabric. It found that nail holes repaired with materials vulcanized to the inside of the tire were satisfactory, on the condition that the holes are permanently sealed on the inside.
  • Chafer fabric. Exposed ply cord would bar retreading, but exposed chafer fabric, the special fabric placed around the bead, is permitted.
  • Edger fabric. NHTSA said adding edger fabric strips is prohibited, because it amounts to adding a belt.

These are interpretations of the regulation's text, not a substitute for it. Check the current regulation and any more recent NHTSA interpretations before you rely on a specific reading.

General repair practice that carries over

Published repair guidance aimed mainly at passenger and light-truck tires makes points that are widely applied across tire repair. Treat it as general background, because truck-casing limits come from the TRMG practice above rather than from this guidance:

  • A plug alone or a patch alone is not an acceptable repair. A one-piece repair unit combines a stem and a patch portion.
  • The tire must be removed from the wheel and inspected internally before any repair, because a technician cannot inspect the inside of a tire that is still on the rim.
  • Repair units cannot overlap.
  • The tire manufacturer's repair policy comes first, then the application and the condition found at inspection.

What it means for an in-house retread line

When casings go to a third-party retreader, repair decisions are made in their shop. When you run your own line, the repair step is yours to set up and document. Our reasoning, not a published figure:

  • Write the repair procedure down. Base it on the current TRMG recommended practices and the tread manufacturer's requirements, and keep the documents on hand for your own technicians.
  • Inspect first, repair second. A spreader inspection finds injuries that are not visible from the outside. Our inspection spreader is built for that step.
  • Match tools to injury type. Skiving tools such as carbide cutters, rotary gouges and white oxide stones prepare the injury, and an extruder gun fills it. See our extruder gun buyer's guide.
  • Reject, don't rescue, structural damage. Bead-area penetration or exposed ply cord is a scrap decision under the sources above, not a repair decision.
  • Track repairs per casing. Repair history feeds your casing plan and your casing salvage rate.
