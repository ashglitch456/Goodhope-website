Bead-to-Bead vs. Top-Cap Retreading: Choosing the Right Process for Fleet Tires

Top-cap and bead-to-bead precure retreads cover different parts of the casing. What each protects, when a shop should choose one over the other, and what it means for equipment.

Published September 19, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

Once a shop has settled on precure retreading — the process our pre-cure vs. mold-cure guide covers in detail — there's a second, narrower decision that still has to be made on every casing: how much of the tire actually gets new rubber. A top cap puts precured tread on the crown only, right where the tire meets the road. A full cap extends that precured rubber down over the shoulders. Bead-to-bead goes further still, renovating the tread and the sidewall down toward the bead. Same curing chamber, same basic building process — different footprint of new rubber, and a different set of reasons to pick one.

This is a real, distinct call from mold-cure vs. precure — it's a question shops make within precure, for every casing that comes through inspection. Here's what each option actually covers, when it makes sense, and what it changes about the equipment and consumables you run.

What each process actually covers

Industry terminology on this isn't perfectly standardized between suppliers, but the underlying distinction is consistent and shows up the same way across trade sources: how far down the casing the new precured rubber extends.

Top cap (top treading)

Only the rubber on the tread area — the part that contacts the road — is replaced. The buffed casing is prepped down to the crown, a strip of cushion gum goes on, and a precured tread rubber strip is applied over it and cured in the chamber. The shoulders and sidewalls are left as they are. It's the smaller, faster, lower-material job, and for a casing whose only wear is in the tread, it's also the more efficient one.

Full cap (full treading)

Full treading replaces the rubber over the shoulder as well as the crown — both the shoulder and upper sidewall area of the casing are buffed and covered with new precured rubber. It's the middle option: more coverage and more casing protection than a top cap, without going all the way down to the bead.

Bead-to-bead

Bead-to-bead retreading replaces the worn tread and renovates the sidewall rubber as well, extending coverage down toward — or in some builds, all the way to — the bead area. It's the most comprehensive of the three, and it shows up most often where sidewalls take real damage: Goodyear markets its RT-3B off-the-road retread specifically as a bead-to-bead product, describing it as buffed and cured with new rubber "bead-to-bead" for loader and grader applications where cuts and gouges reach into the sidewall, not just the tread.

When each one makes sense

Casing condition drives the decision more than anything else

Every casing goes through the same inspection regardless of which cap it ends up with — our 5-point casing inspection guide covers that in full. What that inspection turns up is what decides the cap:

  • Tread wear only, sound shoulders and sidewalls. A steer- or drive-position casing off a highway fleet that's worn evenly and shows no curb rash, cuts, or sidewall scuffing is a textbook top-cap candidate — there's no damage outside the tread area to justify buffing and re-rubbering the shoulder.
  • Shoulder wear or minor shoulder damage. Irregular shoulder wear, light curb contact, or shoulder-area repairs push a casing toward a full cap, which buffs and recovers that zone instead of leaving repaired or worn shoulder rubber exposed under the new tread's edge.
  • Sidewall cuts, curb damage, or severe-service scarring. Where sidewall damage is visible — common on regional and urban routes with curb contact, refuse and construction applications, and off-road/severe-service equipment — bead-to-bead is what actually addresses it, because it's the only option that re-covers the sidewall itself rather than just the tread and shoulder.

Application matters as much as condition

Long-haul steer and drive casings that spend their life on the highway tend to wear predictably in the tread and rarely need more than a top cap. Regional, urban pickup-and-delivery, and refuse routes see far more curb contact and sidewall scuffing over a casing's life, which is exactly the kind of wear a full cap or bead-to-bead is built to cover. Off-road and severe-service equipment — the kind Goodyear's RT-3B and similar OTR bead-to-bead products target — routinely takes cuts that reach into the sidewall, which is why bead-to-bead is the standard option in that segment rather than the exception.

Trailer positions are a slightly different case: because they don't carry drive torque, a trailer casing on its second or later retread cycle is often a reasonable top-cap candidate even when a comparable drive casing wouldn't be, since it's carrying less stress and typically running a shallower tread design built for free-rolling wear.

Equipment and consumables implications

Whichever cap you're running, the equipment stack is the same as any precure line — a tire builder, cushion gum, precured tread rubber, and a curing chamber. What changes is how those inputs get used:

  • Buffing scope. A top cap only needs the crown trued up. A full cap or bead-to-bead means buffing extends over the shoulder — and for bead-to-bead, down the sidewall — which is more buffer time and more buffed rubber (and dust) per casing.
  • Tread rubber and cushion gum footage. A wider precured strip and a wider cushion gum layer are needed to cover the shoulder or sidewall area, meaning more consumable footage consumed per tire as coverage extends — a real per-unit cost difference even though trade sources don't publish a standard percentage for it.
  • Building-station setup. The tire builder and extruder gun need to stitch the wider strip down evenly over a contoured shoulder or sidewall profile rather than a flat crown, which takes more care at the building station to avoid trapped air at the shoulder.
  • Envelope and rim setup. Extending rubber toward the bead means the curing envelope and expandable hub/rim setup have to seal properly around a different rubber profile — one more reason accurate rim and flange sizing matters more, not less, on a full-cap or bead-to-bead build.

So which should your shop run?

For most independent and fleet-service shops, the honest answer is: both, depending on the casing. Top cap should be your default for sound highway casings where inspection turns up nothing beyond tread wear — it's the faster, lower-material job and there's no reason to buff and re-rubber a shoulder that doesn't need it. Full cap and bead-to-bead earn their place on casings where inspection finds shoulder or sidewall damage worth protecting — which, on regional, urban, and severe-service fleets, is a meaningful share of what comes through the door.

The equipment doesn't change between the two — the same buffer, builder, cushion gum, tread rubber, and curing chamber handle either job. What matters is stocking wide enough tread rubber and cushion gum to cover both, and training the building station to handle a shoulder or sidewall profile, not just a flat crown.
