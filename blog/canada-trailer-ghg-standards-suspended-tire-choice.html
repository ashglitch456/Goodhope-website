Canada Has Paused Its Trailer GHG Standards Again: What It Means for Trailer Tires and Retreads

An interim order made in October keeps the federal trailer standards, and the low-rolling-resistance tire requirement inside them, out of force. Trailer tire choices remain a fleet's own economic decision.

Published November 20, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

On October 13, 2026, the federal Minister of the Environment made another interim order suspending the greenhouse gas emission standards for trailers in Canada's Heavy-duty Vehicle and Engine Greenhouse Gas Emission Regulations. The notice was published in the Canada Gazette, Part I, on October 31. Its operative text says the regulations do not apply to trailers whose manufacture is completed on or after January 1, 2020, and it repeals the previous order, made on November 24, 2025.

This has been the pattern since 2019. Canada's trailer standards were written in 2018, were due to start with trailers built from 2020, and have been suspended by a run of interim orders, usually renewed about once a year, ever since. In practice they have never taken effect. The latest order matters to fleets because one of the things those standards would have required was low-rolling-resistance tires on many new trailers.

What the trailer standards would have required

The 2018 amendments to the regulations (SOR/2018-98), part of Canada's "Phase 2" heavy-duty rules, added trailers hauled by on-road tractors as a newly regulated category. The standards were set to tighten in steps up to the 2027 model year and stay at that level afterwards. For many box vans, compliance was design-based: the trailer had to carry low-rolling-resistance tires and either a tire pressure monitoring system or an automatic tire inflation system. Rolling resistance was capped in kilograms per tonne, measured by the U.S. EPA test procedure. For example, the archived regulation text set a cap of 6 kg/t or less for non-box trailers in the 2020 model year.

Two points about scope are worth keeping straight:

  • The rules targeted new trailers. They applied to trailers by the date their manufacture was completed, as obligations on the companies that build or import them. They did not govern the tires a fleet fits later in a trailer's life.
  • The suspension covers trailers only. The October order deals only with the trailer standards. The rest of the regulations, which cover heavy-duty vehicles and engines, are a separate matter.

Why Canada keeps pausing them

The government's stated reason is alignment with the United States. The U.S. adopted parallel trailer standards in its 2016 Phase 2 rule, but on November 12, 2021, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit vacated the trailer portions in Truck Trailer Manufacturers Association v. EPA. The court held that trailers are not self-propelled and so are not "motor vehicles" that EPA can regulate under the Clean Air Act. It also set aside the matching fuel-efficiency standards. Earlier Canadian notices said the suspensions give the government time to assess the economic effects of enforcing trailer standards in Canada alone, with no U.S. equivalent.

The suspension has a cost the government puts in writing. A February 2025 Gazette notice estimated that suspending the standards for one more model year would reduce expected emission reductions by about 0.5 megatonnes of CO2e over the lifetime of model-year 2026 trailers. It put the total for model years 2020 to 2026 at about 3.1 megatonnes.

What it means for trailer tire decisions

For a fleet, the practical result is simple. No federal rule tells you what rolling resistance your trailer tires must have, or that a new trailer must come with tire pressure monitoring. Those choices remain economic. Low rolling resistance and good inflation pay for themselves in fuel and casing life, so the business case for them never depended on a mandate.

  • Rolling resistance still costs fuel. EPA's SmartWay program verifies low-rolling-resistance new tires and retread technologies. It reports that certain LRR tires and retreads can cut fuel costs and emissions for long-haul Class 8 tractor-trailers by 3% or more. That figure assumes verified retreads on both drive and trailer axles, verified tires on the steer axle, and proper inflation. EPA also reports smaller savings when LRR tires are used only on the trailer. See our SmartWay guide for the detail.
  • Retreads belong on the trailer. Under SmartWay, retread technologies are verified for exactly two positions: drive and trailer. We cover how tread choice differs by position in tread selection by axle position.
  • Inflation monitoring is still worth it. The standards would have required TPMS or automatic inflation because underinflation wastes fuel and damages casings. That remains true without a rule. Our article on underinflation and casing life covers the evidence.

One practical point for fleets that buy or spec retreads. A low-rolling-resistance trailer program is easier to manage when you control the tread you put on your own casings. A fleet running its own line chooses the tread compound and design for each casing. It is not limited to whatever an outside retreader stocks.

What to watch

Earlier orders in this series followed a pattern: each lapsed about a year after it was made unless it was repealed or replaced first. We have not confirmed the exact expiry terms of the October 2026 order. Anyone who builds, imports or specs new trailers should read the full notice in the Canada Gazette and watch for any amendment to the regulations themselves. If the U.S. position changes, Canada's reason for suspending its standards could change with it. We will update this article if that happens.
