The Tax Case for Bringing Retreading In-House: How CCA Affects Your Equipment Investment

Fleets weighing a retread line usually price the equipment and the fuel/tire savings. They rarely price the depreciation treatment — and a major federal change just announced in September 2026 makes this the moment to look at it.

Published September 19, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

This is not tax advice. It's a plain-language explainer of real, current CRA and Finance Canada rules, sourced below, so you can bring the right questions to your own accountant. We're a manufacturer-direct equipment and consumables supplier, not an accounting firm — the specifics of your situation need a professional who can see your actual books.

We've written before about what a retread line actually costs to set up and the inputs that determine whether it pays for itself. Both of those posts talk about the sticker price of equipment and the per-tire savings it generates. Neither talks about a third variable that changes the economics again: how quickly the tax system lets you write that equipment off. That's a genuinely under-discussed piece of the in-house decision — and as of this month, it just changed in a big way.

The baseline: how CCA works for retread equipment

When a corporation buys equipment — a curing chamber, a tire builder, a buffing machine, an extruder — it can't usually deduct the full purchase price against income in the year it's bought. Instead, the Income Tax Act lets you deduct a prescribed percentage of the equipment's cost each year, on a declining-balance basis, through Capital Cost Allowance (CCA). CRA groups depreciable property into numbered classes, each with its own rate, and machinery and equipment used primarily to manufacture or process goods for sale generally falls into Class 43, which carries a 30% declining-balance rate. In a normal year, that's also subject to the "half-year rule," which limits the CCA claim on new additions to half the normal rate in the year the asset is acquired, on the reasoning that it wasn't in use for the full year.

There's an open question worth flagging honestly rather than glossing over: whether tire retreading equipment specifically qualifies as "manufacturing or processing" property (Class 43) versus general-purpose machinery in a different class is a classification call that depends on the specifics of your operation, and we couldn't find CRA guidance that addresses tire retreading by name. CRA's Income Tax Folio S4-F15-C1 sets out the general manufacturing-and-processing test, but applying it to your shop is exactly the kind of thing to confirm with your accountant before you file — not something to assume from a blog post.

The faster track that's been available: Class 53 and the Accelerated Investment Incentive

Since 2018, Ottawa has run a series of measures specifically to speed up write-offs on manufacturing and processing equipment, on top of the standard Class 43 rate. Equipment acquired and available for use after 2015 and before 2026 could instead go into Class 53, a faster 50% declining-balance class built for M&P machinery. Layered on top of that, the Accelerated Investment Incentive (AII) — introduced for property acquired after November 20, 2018 — suspended the half-year rule and, for M&P equipment specifically, allowed a much larger first-year deduction: multiple professional accounting sources describe this as full (100%) first-year expensing for M&P equipment made available for use before 2024, stepping down to 75% for property available for use in 2024–2025, and 55% for 2026–2027, before reverting to standard treatment. After 2025, new M&P equipment additions stop qualifying for Class 53 altogether and fall back into the general Class 43 pool.

Practically, that meant a retreading line bought in the last several years could often be written off much faster than the plain 30% Class 43 rate implies — which is exactly the kind of detail that changes a payback calculation but rarely shows up in an equipment-cost blog post. It's also a stepped, multi-year schedule, so the exact percentage that applies depends on the calendar year the equipment becomes "available for use" — a defined term that generally means when it's installed and ready to operate, not just invoiced. That's one more reason the timing of a purchase and commissioning date is worth planning with your accountant, not just your equipment supplier.

What just changed: the Productivity Mega Deduction (announced September 15, 2026)

This is genuinely new, and it's the reason this topic is worth revisiting right now. On September 15, 2026, the federal government announced draft legislative proposals for a new measure it's calling the "Productivity Mega Deduction" — a permanent, broad-based immediate-expensing regime. As described in Finance Canada's announcement and corroborated by multiple independent tax and law firm summaries published the same week, the measure would let businesses deduct 100% of the capital cost of a much wider range of depreciable property — including manufacturing and processing machinery and equipment — in the same tax year it's acquired and becomes available for use, rather than spreading the deduction over years through CCA. Coverage describes it as expanding the share of business capital investment eligible for full, immediate write-off from roughly 15% to more than 65%, with the government citing an estimated fiscal cost of about $36 billion over five years starting in 2026–27. The measure is described as applying to eligible property acquired and available for use after September 14, 2026.

Two important caveats. First, as of this post's publish date, this is proposed legislation, not enacted law — it was announced and put out in draft form days before we wrote this, and it still has to go through the normal legislative process before it's final. Treat it as a strong signal of direction, not a rule you can rely on for this year's filing until it's passed. Second, we could not independently verify every detail of how it interacts with the existing Class 43/53 and AII rules described above — some technical points (exact eligible-property definitions, treatment of used equipment, any anti-avoidance restrictions) will only be settled once the legislation is finalized. If you're timing a retread-line purchase around this, confirm the current status directly with your accountant or with CRA/Finance Canada guidance before you commit — don't plan a purchase solely on a announcement that hasn't become law yet.

For context: the CCPC immediate-expensing measure that came before it

This isn't the first time Ottawa has tried immediate expensing. A narrower measure let Canadian-controlled private corporations (CCPCs) — the structure most independent fleet operators and family-owned trucking businesses use — immediately expense up to $1.5 million of eligible property per year, shared among associated corporations, for property acquired after April 18, 2021. That measure expired for CCPCs at the end of 2023 and no longer applies. It's useful background because it shows the pattern: government has repeatedly reached for immediate expensing as an investment incentive, then let it lapse or narrowed it — which is one more reason to confirm current-year status rather than rely on what a program did in a prior year.

Why this changes the payback math — not the savings, the timing

Our ROI post lays out the operational side of the payback calculation: retreads-per-year, savings-per-retread, cycles-per-casing, and equipment cost. None of that changes here. What changes is when the tax benefit of the equipment purchase actually shows up in your cash flow. A dollar of CCA deduction claimed this year is worth more than the same dollar of CCA spread out and claimed five years from now, because the tax saving arrives sooner and money has a time value. Faster depreciation — whether that's the existing Class 53/AII treatment or, if it becomes law, immediate expensing under the Productivity Mega Deduction — doesn't change the sticker price of the equipment or the retread economics from our ROI post. It changes how fast the after-tax cost of that sticker price is realized, which shortens the effective payback period on top of the operational savings, not instead of them.

Here's a purely illustrative example to show the mechanism — not a real fleet's numbers, and not a projection for your business. Say a fleet buys $200,000 CAD of retread equipment (a hypothetical figure, chosen for round numbers — see our actual equipment cost breakdown for real ranges), and assume, purely for illustration, a combined federal-and-Ontario corporate tax rate of roughly 25% — your actual rate depends on your corporate structure and income level, and should be confirmed with your accountant. Under standard Class 43 CCA with the half-year rule, only 15% of that cost (half the 30% rate) is deductible in year one — a $30,000 deduction, worth about $7,500 in tax savings that year, with the rest of the write-off, and the rest of the tax benefit, spread over many subsequent years. Under 100% immediate expensing, the entire $200,000 is deductible in year one — a $50,000 tax saving realized immediately, instead of trickling in over a decade-plus of declining-balance claims. The total tax saved over the equipment's life is similar either way; what changes is how much of it lands in year one, which is what actually moves a payback-period calculation. That's the mechanism — apply your own equipment cost and your own accountant-confirmed tax rate to see what it means for you.

Not tax advice — confirm the specifics before you plan around them

To be explicit: everything above is general information about published CRA and Finance Canada rules, sourced as of September 2026, written to help you ask better questions — not a determination of how these rules apply to your business. Before you time an equipment purchase around any of this, confirm with your own accountant or tax advisor: (1) whether your specific equipment qualifies as manufacturing-and-processing property for CCA classification purposes, (2) the current-year enhanced first-year rate that applies given the stepped AII phase-down schedule, (3) whether the Productivity Mega Deduction has been enacted into law by the time you're filing, and (4) your corporation's actual applicable tax rate and structure. We supply and service the equipment; your accountant confirms how the tax system treats it.
