Reading Irregular Wear: What Tire Wear Patterns Tell an In-House Retread Line

Feather, shoulder, center and flat-spot wear each point to a different cause, and some still leave a retreadable casing. What the trade press says, and how a fleet running its own line can use it.

Published October 21, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

A worn-out tire is also a report card. The way the tread has worn tells you whether the truck was out of alignment, whether the tire was run over- or under-inflated, or whether a worn shock or bearing was chewing it up. For a fleet that retreads its own casings, that report card matters twice: it points to a maintenance fix that protects the next casing, and it helps decide whether this one still goes back on the line.

This guide summarises what the trade press and tire-maker guidance say about common wear patterns and what to do with the casing. It is deliberately light on numbers. The sources we could reach this run describe causes and dispositions, not measured savings, so we are not publishing a dollar figure.

A note on sourcing

We could not open several of the primary pages from our research environment, so the descriptions below reflect what the search results reported from each publisher. Check each against the original before you build a policy on it, and treat your own casing inspection as the deciding step, not a wear label.

Common wear patterns and what the sources say

  • Feather wear. A Fleet Maintenance guide to commercial truck tire wear describes feather wear as steer-tire ribs worn high to low across each rib, usually from too much toe on the steer axle or drive-axle misalignment. For feathering, its disposition is to rotate the tire to another position or retread it.
  • One-sided or shoulder wear. The same guide ties outside-shoulder wear heavier than inside to excessive toe-in, and heavy inside-shoulder wear to excessive toe-out. It says excessive camber wears one shoulder quickly. Michelin’s business guidance likewise attributes one-sided wear to alignment parameters such as camber, toe and axle parallelism.
  • Shoulder depression wear. Per the Fleet Maintenance guide, this is usually a shock, bearing, mismount or balance problem rather than alignment, and its guidance is to continue running to the pull point and then retread.
  • Center wear and both-shoulder wear. Michelin’s consumer tire guidance links center-tread wear to chronic overinflation and wear on both outer edges to chronic underinflation. The Fleet Maintenance guide also lists rapid shoulder wear on both drive tires as linked to light loads.
  • Diagonal flat spots. The Fleet Maintenance guide lists improper bearing adjustment, misalignment and mismatched dual diameter or inflation among the probable causes.
  • Brake skid damage. Per the same guide, the tire may be repaired or retreaded if the casing is undamaged, and is scrapped if not.

The pattern in those dispositions is consistent: most wear patterns are a tread-surface symptom of a mechanical or inflation cause, and the casing decision depends on whether the casing itself is undamaged. That is why the answer always comes back to inspection (see our 5-point casing check) and to your pull-point policy.

The industry reference for wear analysis

The Technology & Maintenance Council (TMC) of the American Trucking Associations publishes a Radial Tire Conditions Analysis Guide. Modern Tire Dealer, Trailer/Body Builders and European Rubber Journal each reported on its editions: the guide covers casing conditions on original and retreaded tires, failed repairs in the tread area, and wear conditions and causes for steer, drive and trailer tires. The fourth edition was reported as including more than 200 colour photographs and illustrations, covering irregular wear, diagonal wear, shoulder wear and sidewall separations. Those reports date from roughly 2005 to 2012, so confirm the current edition and how it sets retread-or-scrap criteria with TMC directly. The coverage we found did not say how the guide makes that call.

Why alignment keeps coming up

Heavy Duty Trucking’s alignment explainer cites a TMC position that improper toe settings are the most common misalignment problem and have the greatest effect on truck tire wear, and says toe is usually set to about 1/16 inch of toe-in. Commercial Carrier Journal reports tire-manufacturer estimates of a 1–2 percent fuel-economy penalty from even slight misalignment. Those are publisher-reported figures, not our measurements, and the effect on your fleet depends on your own trucks and routes.

What an in-house line can do with this

This section is reasoning, not data. A fleet that runs its own line sees the casing at every step: pulled at the yard, inspected, buffed and finished. That puts wear information and casing outcome in the same hands, which makes a simple feedback loop possible:

  • Record the wear pattern at intake. Add a wear-pattern field to your casing record, next to position and removal reason.
  • Trace patterns to units and positions. A cluster of feathered steer tires on the same few trucks points to an alignment or axle problem to fix, not a tire problem.
  • Act on inflation patterns. Center wear and both-shoulder wear are inflation signals; see underinflation and casing life.
  • Watch duals. Diagonal wear on duals is a prompt to check diameter and inflation matching; see dual matching.
  • Prepare the surface for what you find. Uneven wear changes how much rubber the buffer has to remove; see buffing profile and crown radius.

The point is independence of information: when you run the line, the reject reasons and wear patterns are yours to analyse, whatever supplier you buy tread rubber and consumables from. We are not claiming a specific saving from doing so.

What we are not claiming

  • We are not publishing a fleet example, a casing-yield improvement or a dollar figure. None of our sources measured one.
  • Vendor-blog figures for tire-life loss from misalignment came up in our search and are not repeated here, because they cite no primary test.
  • A wear label does not make a casing retreadable or scrap. Only inspection of the casing does.
