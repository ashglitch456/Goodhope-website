How Much Tread Rubber Does a Retread Use? Sizing Your Stock From MTD Averages

Modern Tire Dealer ranks U.S. retreaders by tread rubber used and publishes per-tire averages: about 12 lb for a light truck retread, 24 lb for a truck retread, 324 lb for OTR. What those numbers mean for an in-house line.

Published October 18, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

Ask a fleet that is thinking about its own retread line how much tread rubber it will need, and the honest first answer is usually a guess. There is a published starting point, though. Modern Tire Dealer (MTD) ranks the largest U.S. retreaders every year by the amount of tread rubber they use, and to compare plants that build very different tires, it converts output into pounds of rubber using fixed per-tire averages.

Those averages are useful for a first-pass estimate. They are also easy to over-trust. This article shows how to use them, and where to stop.

What MTD publishes

MTD’s 2026 Top 50 U.S. Retreaders list ranks companies by total tread rubber usage, which lets it compare output across tire segments. Its method assumes an average of about 12 pounds of rubber for a light truck retread, 24 pounds for a medium/heavy truck retread and 324 pounds for an off-the-road (OTR) retread. Each retread then earns one point per seven pounds of rubber, which works out to roughly 1.7 points for a light truck tire, 3.5 for a truck tire and 46 for an OTR tire. The same averages appear on MTD’s 2025 and 2021 lists, so they are a long-standing convention rather than a one-year figure.

For scale, MTD’s 2025 list put the largest retreader at roughly 56 million pounds of rubber used. That is a multi-plant operation. A fleet line sits at the other end of the scale, which is exactly why a per-tire average is the more useful number for you.

Turning the averages into a first-pass estimate

The arithmetic is simple. This is our own calculation from MTD’s averages, not a forecast and not a supplier specification:

  • 500 truck retreads a year × 24 lb ≈ 12,000 lb of tread rubber a year.
  • 2,000 truck retreads a year × 24 lb ≈ 48,000 lb a year, or roughly 960 lb a week over 50 working weeks.
  • A light truck mix at 12 lb per tire needs about half the rubber per tire, but usually more tires to reach the same revenue or fleet coverage.

One OTR retread at 324 lb uses as much rubber as thirteen average truck retreads (13.5, to be exact). A line that adds even a small amount of OTR work changes its rubber demand more than the tire count suggests. Our guide to OTR versus truck and bus retreading covers how different those two jobs are.

Where the averages stop being reliable

MTD’s figures are ranking conventions, built so that a list of 50 companies can be compared fairly. They are not a purchasing spec. Before you order rubber, expect real consumption to differ for reasons the average hides:

  • Tread design and width. Wider, deeper designs carry more rubber per tire than a shallow linehaul tread.
  • Process. Pre-cure tread strips and mold-cure uncured rubber are bought and measured differently. See our comparison of pre-cure and mold-cure retreading.
  • Buffing and trimming waste. Not every pound bought ends up on a tire.
  • Cushion gum and repair compound. These are separate consumables and are not part of the tread rubber average.

The practical rule: use MTD’s numbers to size the order of magnitude, then replace them with the tread weight per tire that your own tread supplier gives for your sizes and designs. After a few months of production, your own usage records are the number that matters.

Why this planning problem looks different when the line is yours

This is reasoning rather than data. When a fleet relies on an outside retreader, tread rubber is the retreader’s inventory problem. When the line is in-house, it becomes yours: you decide how much to hold, which designs to stock and how to rotate it. Tread rubber and cushion gum are uncured or time-sensitive materials, so overstocking has a cost too. We cover that in our guide to tread rubber and cushion gum shelf life.

It also shifts the supplier relationship. A line that knows its own weekly rubber consumption can order on its own schedule from any supplier that can meet the specification. Our piece on single-supplier lock-in versus running your own line makes the general case. For the full list of what a line consumes beyond tread rubber, see consumables supply planning for an in-house retread line.

A simple way to start

  • Pick your expected annual retread count by tire class (light truck, truck, OTR).
  • Multiply by MTD’s averages for a ceiling-level rubber estimate.
  • Ask your tread supplier for the actual tread weight for each size and design you will run.
  • Add a margin for buffing and trimming waste and for the shelf-life limits on what you hold.
  • Track real consumption weekly from the first month and re-forecast.
