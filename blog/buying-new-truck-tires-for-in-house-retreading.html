IN-HOUSE RETREADING

Buying New Truck Tires When You Run Your Own Retread Line: Casings, Warranties and Lifetime Cost

Once you retread your own tires, a new tire purchase is also a casing purchase. That changes how you should compare tires, and which promises on the spec sheet you can actually count on.

Published November 20, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

Most tire buying still starts with the price of the new tire. For a fleet that sends worn tires out to be retreaded, that makes some sense, because the casing's later value is partly someone else's problem. A fleet that runs its own retread line does not have that luxury. Every new tire it buys is the raw material for the line's future retreads. A casing that fails inspection is a retread the line never gets to make.

This article covers how to compare new tires on lifetime cost, what trade sources say about budget casings, and what to check before you count on a manufacturer's casing warranty.

Why the casing decides the economics

The casing is where most of a retread's savings come from. Fleet Equipment puts a retread on a retreader's own casing at about half the cost of a new tire, depending on tread design and depth. When the fleet supplies the casing and buys only the tread, the retread costs about a quarter as much as a new tire. The Tire Retread & Repair Information Bureau (TRIB) describes retreads more generally as 30% to 50% cheaper than new tires, depending on fleet size and maintenance costs.

A fleet with its own line is always in that "fleet supplies the casing" position. Its savings depend on how many of its casings survive to be retreaded, and how many times. We cover how to measure that in casing salvage rate and retread cycles per casing.

Compare tires on lifetime cost, not purchase price

Trade guidance on choosing commercial tires now routinely points fleets to total cost of ownership rather than purchase price, and it counts how many times a casing can be retreaded as part of that cost. For a fleet with its own line, the comparison looks like this:

Lifetime cost per mile = (new tire price + in-house cost of each retread − any value left in the final casing) ÷ (miles on the original tread + miles on each retread)

The in-house cost of each retread includes tread rubber, cushion gum, cement, envelope wear, energy and labour. The number of retreads is not a guess. Your own casing inspection records show it for each tire line you run. A cheaper tire that gives one fewer retread, or whose casings fail inspection more often, can lose on this measure even when it wins on purchase price. Our article on cost per mile for retreads and new tires covers the wider numbers.

What trade sources say about budget casings

Trade coverage of commercial tires is consistent on one point. Premium tires are usually described as having better fuel efficiency, durability and retreadability than budget tires, and budget tires' retreadability, durability and mileage may all be lower. Transport Topics reported this in its coverage of how fleets balance tire cost and performance. An NC State supply-chain article on retreading makes a similar point more bluntly. It says retreads can be built on nearly any casing that passes inspection, with one exception: ultra-low-cost import tires, because casing quality matters.

The market is moving both ways. In November 2026, Fleet Maintenance reported commercial tire dealers' view that some fleets are prioritizing cost per mile, casing management and retreadability, while others are moving to lower-priced products to control immediate costs. Dealers also reported more purchases of lower-tier imported tires.

None of this means every lower-priced tire is a bad casing. Most of the published commentary comes from manufacturers and retreaders, and we found no independent test data comparing casing failure rates by brand. The practical answer for a fleet with its own line is to measure it. Track inspection pass rates and retread counts by tire line, and let your own data decide.

Casing warranties: read the conditions

Several major tire manufacturers publish casing warranties on their premium commercial lines. In the warranty documents we reviewed from three of them, coverage on top lines ran to about seven years, with multiple or unlimited retreads. These warranties signal confidence in the casing, and they can be worth having.

Read the conditions before you rely on one. In all three documents, casing coverage depended on the casing being inspected and retreaded by that manufacturer's authorized dealers or retread process. A fleet that retreads on its own line should assume that coverage may not apply to casings it retreads itself, and should ask the manufacturer directly before buying on the strength of the warranty. Terms also vary by tire line, size and type of service, and published documents may be out of date.

This is the same structural point we make about single-supplier lock-in. A benefit that only applies if you keep using one supplier's network is part of that network's price, not a free extra. For a fleet running its own line, the better protection is its own inspection and salvage data, built up casing by casing.

A buying checklist for fleets with their own line

  • Trial before you standardize. Run a new tire line on a defined group of units and follow those casings through at least their first retread before you switch the whole fleet.
  • Record every casing. Keep the DOT/TIN, tire line, position, pull tread depth and the inspection result. Without per-line records you cannot calculate lifetime cost.
  • Pull on time. A good casing run past its pull point can still end up as scrap. Pull points are part of the buying decision.
  • Keep sizes consistent. Fewer sizes and tread widths means less tread and envelope stock to carry. Our consumables planning guide explains why.
  • Check warranty conditions in writing. Ask whether casing coverage survives in-house retreading before you count it in the price comparison.
