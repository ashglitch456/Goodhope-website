Precured Tread Selection: Matching Design, Depth and Width to Each Axle Position

How to choose the right precured tread for drive, trailer and mixed-duty retreads — pattern, tread depth, and width matched to the buffed casing, with what EPA and trade sources actually say.

Published November 16, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

Once a casing passes inspection and buffing, the next decision on a precure line is which tread goes on it. Ask "which tread?" and you're really asking three things: the design (pattern and shoulder type), the depth (how many 32nds of rubber), and the width (how it fits the buffed crown). Get those right for the axle position and the application, and the retread earns its miles. Get them wrong and you can see irregular wear, extra rolling resistance, or a tread that never sits correctly on the casing.

This guide pulls together what trade press, EPA and published tread-maker specifications say about each choice. It's written for shops running their own precure process and for fleets thinking about doing so.

Start with the position cascade

Tread selection begins with where the tire will run next, and that isn't always where it ran before. Fleet Equipment describes a common commercial pattern: steer tires are usually retreaded the first time with a drive design. Drive tires are retreaded as drive tires, then go back to a trailer design. Trailer tires come back as trailer treads.

The same article gives a line-haul example that shows why the cascade matters. A new drive tire with 30/32 of tread rubber can reach about 350,000 miles with strong maintenance. A drive retread with 22/32 of rubber can add 200,000-plus miles, and a later trailer retread about 125,000 more. Those are illustrative figures from one trade-press source, not a guarantee. But they show the logic: each tread choice is set for the position the casing is moving into.

Steer is usually left out of the retread menu by fleet policy, and in some cases by regulation. In the US, 49 CFR 393.75 bars regrooved, recapped or retreaded tires on the front wheels of a bus. Canada's rules are stricter than the US's. We cover both in Retreads on the Steer Axle. EPA's SmartWay program likewise verifies retread technologies only for the drive and trailer positions.

Design: what the pattern has to do in each position

Drive treads: traction first, then wear and fuel

Drive tires transmit torque, so drive treads carry deeper lugs or blocks than trailer treads. The main design fork is the shoulder:

  • Closed-shoulder drive treads. A continuous shoulder rib. Tire Review lists better fuel economy among the closed-shoulder advantages, and trade press generally points them at long-haul tractors where even wear matters most.
  • Open-shoulder drive treads. Lugs that run out through the shoulder. Fleet Equipment notes these are typically used in local or on/off-road service for traction. On a long-haul tractor they can be prone to irregular shoulder wear, and because long-haul tires wear slowly, that wear has more time to develop.

Rule of thumb from the sources: closed shoulder for highway miles, open shoulder where traction in mud, snow or job-site conditions is worth a wear trade-off. Inflation and alignment discipline matter either way. See our post on underinflation and casing life.

Trailer treads: shallow ribs that resist scrub

Trailer tires roll free. They don't steer and they don't take torque. Trade press notes that this is why trailer designs tend to be shallow and rib-type: a rib keeps more of the tread face on the road, which helps wear rate and resistance to irregular wear. One tire engineer quoted by Overdrive put it more bluntly: trailer tires use shallower tread because deep tread there tends to wear irregularly.

The exception is high-scrub service: spread-axle trailers, tight urban turns, frequent curbing. Here trade guidance favours solid, full-depth shoulder ribs and scrub-resistant compounds. It also warns that deeper treads with decoupled or undercut shoulder ribs can tear in high-scrub tandem service. If your trailers run spread axles, select treads built for that duty rather than a generic line-haul rib.

All-position treads

Some tread makers publish all-position designs at moderate depth (one major retread network lists an all-position tread at 17/32). They simplify inventory for mixed fleets, but they're a compromise. Use them where the duty cycle really is mixed, not as a default.

Depth: more rubber isn't automatically more value

Published precure spec sheets show how far depth varies by position. Trailer treads from one major tread maker are listed around 11/32. Drive treads run deeper: published examples include 19/32 or 23/32 depending on tread width for one maker's drive design, and 26/32 for another's deep drive tread.

Deeper tread buys removal miles, but it costs fuel and heat:

  • Rolling resistance. A Michelin engineer quoted in trade press said deeper tread and more open designs tend to have higher rolling resistance than shallower, more closed designs, and put the tread at about 40% of a tire's rolling resistance. Other trade sources say the casing accounts for more than half, so treat the split as approximate. The direction isn't disputed.
  • Heat. Fleet Equipment notes that shallower treads generate less heat than deeper ones. Lower running temperature is good for fuel and for the casing you plan to retread again.
  • Why it matters. EPA SmartWay estimates tire rolling resistance at nearly 13% of a combination truck's energy use.
  • The trade-off. TMC's Peggy Fisher has noted that because low-rolling-resistance tires usually carry less tread depth, they will still run fewer miles to wear-out. Manufacturers argue the fuel savings and better compounds narrow that gap.

Even tread makers argue for shallow in some cases. One major tread maker says of one of its drive treads that a shallower depth means less weight, less irregular wear and less cost. The right depth depends on the application: line-haul fleets chasing fuel economy lean shallower, while vocational and regional fleets that wear tread faster often lean deeper.

If fuel economy is a buying criterion, check the tread against the SmartWay verified list. EPA's 2022 retread verification protocol lists rolling-resistance targets of 6.6 for drive and 5.1 for trailer retreads. EPA's requirements also say a model verified at its deepest tread depth covers shallower depths of that model, but not the other way round.

Width: match the tread to the buffed crown

Width is the choice most often got wrong on a new line, because it depends on what happened at the buffer. Patent literature on precured treads (US 5,277,727, "Contoured replacement tread") says plainly that precured treads are relatively intolerant of variation in crown arc width and radius. When the tread and the buffed crown don't match, stresses build in the retread assembly and hurt both operation and longevity. The same document recommends that the buffed crown width approximate the tread width of the new tire the casing came from. It also notes that over-buffing to clean up shoulder voids can leave a crown that's too narrow.

In practice:

  • Buff to the tread maker's specification for the tread width you intend to apply, not to a generic profile.
  • Stock the widths your casing mix actually needs. A single width across many casing sizes saves inventory but compromises fit.
  • Measure after buffing. Crown width and radius are checks, not assumptions. A consistent buffing machine setup and correct rasp selection make the target repeatable.

Why this matters more on your own line

When a fleet sends casings out, tread choice is often whatever the outside retreader stocks. When you run the line yourself, you choose: design by application, depth by your fuel-versus-mileage priorities, width matched to your own buffing. You can track the result too. Combine tread choice with sensible pull points and you control most of what decides cost per mile.
