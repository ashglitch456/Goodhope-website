Nail-Hole Repair Limits: What Keeps a Truck Casing Retreadable

Crown-only, 3/8-inch limit, stem and patch together: what published nail-hole repair guidance says about which punctures are repairable, and how an in-house shop should protect casings for retreading.

Published October 9, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

Every casing that reaches your retread line has a history, and for many casings that history includes at least one puncture repair. Whether that repair was done correctly decides whether the casing is still worth retreading. Published guidance is consistent on the main limits: where the injury is, how big it is, and what goes into the hole.

This article summarizes that guidance as reported by trade press and tire-maker repair procedures. It is a planning aid, not a substitute for the current RMA/TIA nail-hole repair documents or your repair-material manufacturer’s instructions, which control the actual repair.

Where the injury is: crown only

Modern Tire Dealer, summarizing the RMA/TIA position, reports that puncture repairs are limited to the crown area — the center of the tread, roughly 1 to 1.5 inches in from each shoulder. Sidewall and shoulder injuries are not repaired with the standard nail-hole procedure; a tire with one is either referred for a different type of repair or scrapped, depending on the injury and the manufacturer’s policy.

For a retread line this is the first gate. A casing arriving with a shoulder or sidewall injury is a different conversation from one with a crown nail hole, and your intake inspection should sort them accordingly. Our 5-point casing check covers where that sorting fits.

How big: the 3/8-inch line

For medium truck tires, the same trade-press summaries and the PREMA repair wall chart give a maximum nail-hole diameter of 3/8 inch (10 mm). The PREMA chart gives a lower figure for passenger and light truck tires: over 1/4 inch (6 mm) is out of scope. Injuries larger than 3/8 inch on a truck tire are referred to a full-service repair facility for a section repair rather than treated as a nail hole.

Note that the limit applies to the injury after it has been probed and prepared, not to the object that caused it. The Michelin truck nail-hole procedure begins by marking the injury inside and out, removing the object, and probing the channel to check for air infiltration or heavy rust before deciding whether the repair can proceed.

What goes in the hole: stem and patch, together

Both Michelin’s truck nail-hole procedure and the PREMA chart state that a stem or plug alone, or a patch alone, is not an acceptable repair; both must be used, either as a two-piece unit or a one-piece combined unit. Modern Tire Dealer describes the failure mode of patch-only repairs: moisture can follow the unfilled injury channel to the steel belts and body cables, where rust grows and can eventually cause a belt separation.

That failure mode is exactly what costs a casing its next retread. A casing with a patch-only repair may look fine at intake and fail late, which is why repair history belongs on your inspection list.

Preparation details worth writing into your procedure

  • Drilling speed. Michelin’s procedure calls for a carbide cutter run at a low speed, 1,200 rpm or less, working from the inside out and then from the outside, to remove damaged steel and rubber without enlarging the injury more than necessary.
  • Cement the channel. The procedure applies chemical vulcanizing fluid to the channel with a spiral cement tool and lets it set before the stem is installed.
  • Don’t expose plies when buffing the liner. Michelin’s procedure says that if body plies are damaged or exposed during buffing, the tire should not be repaired.
  • Keep materials from one manufacturer. The PREMA chart says repair products should come from the same manufacturer to ensure they are compatible in the curing process.

How many repairs is too many?

There is no single universal count for truck tires. Modern Tire Dealer quotes the RMA wall chart as saying the number of repairs may be limited by application, economics and manufacturers’ recommendations, and notes that repair units must not overlap. The same article describes a further concern when two repairs sit directly opposite each other so the same radial body cable is injured twice. The practical answer is to follow the tire manufacturer’s repair policy and set your own written limit on repairs per casing.

Why this matters more when the line is yours

This part is reasoning rather than data. When a fleet sends casings to an outside retreader, the retreader decides which repaired casings are acceptable and the fleet sees the verdict. When the line is in-house, the same team that repairs puncture injuries in service can also be the team that decides what the retread line will accept, so the standard is set once and applied consistently. Casings that were repaired correctly stay in the pool; the ones that weren’t are caught at intake, before you spend a tread on them.

A simple policy covers most of it:

  • Repair only crown-area injuries within the size limit, and scrap or refer the rest.
  • Require a stem-and-patch (or one-piece) unit for every nail-hole repair, and reject casings that show patch-only repairs.
  • Mark and record each repair so the count and location are visible at retread intake.
  • Set a written maximum number of repairs per casing, aligned with the tire manufacturer’s policy.
  • Pair this with a pull-point policy so casings come off the road early enough to be retreadable — see Tire Pull Points.

Source note: the Modern Tire Dealer articles we relied on are from 2008 and 2011, and we could not retrieve the RMA/TIA documents directly when writing this. Treat the figures above as the commonly cited limits and confirm them against current RMA/TIA and manufacturer documents before writing them into a shop procedure.
