Vulcanizing Cement on an In-House Retread Line: Choosing, Storing and Handling It in Ontario

Flammable or non-flammable, brush or spray, dry time and shelf life: what product data and Ontario's WHMIS and flammable-storage rules mean for the cement on your own retread line.

Published November 11, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

Vulcanizing cement (also called bonding or retread cement) is a small line item on a retread shop's consumables list, but it sits right at the bond between a buffed casing and new rubber. It is also, in many formulations, a flammable liquid. If a fleet brings retreading in-house, the cement stops being something the retreader worries about and becomes something your own shop has to choose, store, label and train people on. This guide covers what published product data shows about the choices, and what Ontario's rules say about handling it.

Sourcing note: we read manufacturer product listings, safety data sheets (SDSs) and Ontario guidance through search-result extracts, not the full documents. We name the rules and the type of source for every point, and we give no shop example, because we have no real one to share. Always follow the SDS and technical data sheet for the exact product you buy.

What the cement does, and why prep comes first

Cement is applied to the buffed and repaired casing so the cushion gum and tread bond to it during curing. No cement makes up for bad prep. As our buffing and casing prep guide explains, an under-buffed surface does not hold cement and cushion gum properly. Cement goes on a correctly textured, clean, dry surface, and then it has to dry before building.

The choices: flammable or non-flammable, brush or spray, cure type

Retread cements are sold in several forms, and the product data differs in ways that matter on the shop floor:

  • Solvent-based (flammable) cements. The retread and vulcanizing cement SDSs we found carry the GHS hazard statement H225 (highly flammable liquid and vapour) with the signal word "Danger", plus a skin-irritation warning. One vulcanizing fluid's SDS adds H336 (may cause drowsiness or dizziness), and one includes precautions on static discharge and non-sparking tools.
  • Water-based or non-flammable cements. One manufacturer lists a water-based, temperature-activated vulcanizing and retread cement with a recommended dry time of 10 to 12 minutes and a 3-year shelf life. Non-flammable products change the storage picture, but they still come with an SDS and handling instructions of their own.
  • Brush or spray. Both are common. One manufacturer's spray cement for tread rubber is labelled for heat-and-pressure cures only and "not for room temperature cure conditions", with a shelf life of 1 year from date of manufacture in the unopened container. The same maker's brush cement is listed for both mold-cure and precure retreading and repair.
  • Cure-type compatibility. Because some cements are made for heated cures only, check that the product matches your process (see precure vs. mold cure and electric vs. steam curing chambers).

The pattern across the product data: dry time, shelf life and cure compatibility vary by product, so they belong on your receiving checklist, not in anyone's memory.

What Ontario requires: WHMIS labels, SDSs and training

In Ontario, an employer's WHMIS duties come mainly from Regulation 860 under the Occupational Health and Safety Act. For a shop handling cement, the points that matter most are:

  • Labels and SDSs. Reg. 860 sets out the employer's duties for labels and safety data sheets. A workplace label is required when a product is moved from the supplier's container into another container, which is common when cement is decanted from a large drum into a working can.
  • Keep SDSs current. SDSs must be updated as new information about a product becomes available. The old fixed three-year update cycle no longer applies.
  • Worker education. Training must cover what the SDS contains and what it means, plus procedures for safe use, storage, handling and disposal. It applies to workers who may be exposed, not only those working right beside the product.

Our Ontario in-house retread shop safety guide covers the wider set of workplace rules a new line runs into.

Storing flammable cement

Flammable liquid storage in an Ontario workplace is covered by two sets of rules: section 22 of the Industrial Establishments Regulation (Reg. 851) and Part 4 of the Ontario Fire Code (O. Reg. 213/07). The Ministry of Labour's pre-start health and safety review guideline says that storing more than 235 litres of flammable liquids per fire cabinet contravenes clause 22(3)(b), and that the liquids in cabinets must be in closed containers. Other institutional guidance we found states the cabinet limits differently, and the Fire Code was amended effective January 1, 2026. Treat the 235-litre figure as a starting point to confirm, not a final answer, and check the current text with your local fire department or a qualified advisor before you set up storage.

Practical steps that follow from the rules and the SDSs:

  • Buy pack sizes that match your usage, so open stock does not sit past its shelf life and bulk stock does not push you over storage limits.
  • Keep containers closed when not in use, and store flammable cement in a proper cabinet away from ignition sources, including the buffing area. Our buffing dust guide explains why that area needs its own fire controls.
  • Label every working container you decant into.
  • Rotate stock first-in, first-out and record the date of manufacture on receipt. The same discipline applies to cushion gum and tread rubber (see tread rubber and cushion gum shelf life).
  • Where the shop can use a non-flammable formulation that suits its cure process, consider it. The storage and fire-safety burden is lower, though the SDS still applies.
