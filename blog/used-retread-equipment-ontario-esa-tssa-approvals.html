Buying Used Retread Equipment in Ontario: The Electrical and Pressure-Vessel Approvals to Check First

Before a fleet buys a used curing chamber or buffer for an in-house retread line in Ontario, check two things: electrical approval under ESA rules and pressure-vessel registration with TSSA. What the rules say and a pre-purchase checklist.

Published November 25, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

When a fleet prices out an in-house retread line, used equipment looks like the obvious way to cut the startup bill. A second-hand curing chamber or buffer from a shop that closed can cost a fraction of new. Sometimes that's a good buy. But in Ontario, two approvals decide whether you can legally switch that equipment on: electrical approval under the Electrical Safety Authority (ESA) rules, and pressure-vessel registration with the Technical Standards and Safety Authority (TSSA). Neither one comes with the equipment automatically. If the paperwork is missing, getting it can take time and money you didn't budget for.

This guide sets out what the rules say, what to check before you buy, and where the cost of a "cheap" machine tends to hide. It's general information, not legal or engineering advice. Confirm the details for your own equipment with ESA, TSSA and a licensed electrical contractor before you commit.

Electrical approval: the equipment has to be approved before it's used

Ontario's Product Safety regulation under the Electricity Act, 1998 (O. Reg. 438/07) says no person may use, advertise, display, sell or offer for sale an electrical product unless it has been approved. That applies to the buyer who switches it on, not only to the seller. ESA says it has the authority to stop the sale of unapproved or hazardous electrical products.

Under Ontario's amendments to the Canadian Electrical Code (Rule 2-024), electrical equipment counts as approved in one of three ways:

  • Certified by an accredited certification body, shown by a recognized certification mark on the equipment. ESA publishes the list of marks it accepts, and says only equipment bearing one of those marks is deemed acceptable.
  • Field evaluated by an accredited field evaluation agency, which inspects the specific unit and applies a field evaluation label.
  • Determined by ESA not to present a hazard to persons or property.

The regulation also provides that a product connected to an electrical installation that ESA inspects can be deemed approved if both the installation and the product pass that inspection. That's a route to discuss with ESA and your electrical contractor, not one to assume.

What this means for a used machine

  • Look for the mark first. A buffer, builder or chamber control panel that carries a mark ESA recognizes is the simplest case. Photograph the nameplate and mark before you agree a price.
  • No mark means a field evaluation. Field evaluation agencies are accredited by the Standards Council of Canada, and at least one lists used and retrofitted industrial equipment among the work it does. ESA's guidance says a field evaluation is limited to the essential safety considerations. Expect the evaluator to want access to the machine and its wiring diagrams, and expect that they may require changes before they'll label it.
  • Modifications can matter. If a previous owner rewired a control panel or swapped a motor, the original certification may no longer reflect what's in front of you. Ask what's been changed.

Pressure equipment: registration with TSSA

Curing chambers and some other retread equipment work under pressure, so they can fall under Ontario's boilers and pressure vessels rules, which TSSA administers. Our workplace safety guide covers the general framework. For used vessels, TSSA has a specific guideline, Design Approval of Used and/or Altered Boilers and Pressure Vessels. Its key points:

  • It applies to anyone who intends to install a used boiler or pressure vessel in Ontario.
  • A vessel found in Ontario without a Canadian Registration Number (CRN) needs a design review of the used vessel in its current condition. Once TSSA accepts the design, it issues a registration number for that individual vessel.
  • The vessel's assembly or arrangement drawing must be stamped and signed by a professional engineer licensed in Ontario.

Registration is handled province by province, so a vessel that was registered somewhere else doesn't automatically carry over. Confirm with TSSA whether an existing registration is valid in Ontario before you treat it as covered.

In practice, the cost of a used chamber includes whatever it takes to get it registered: an engineer's drawings and review, any inspection or testing TSSA requires, and the time all of that takes. If the seller can't produce the original data report and registration, price that work in before you make an offer.

A pre-purchase checklist for used retread equipment

This is our own checklist, built from the rules above. It isn't an official list, but it covers the questions that tend to decide whether a used machine is a bargain:

  • Nameplate and marks. Photos of every nameplate, the electrical certification mark, and any field evaluation label.
  • Pressure documentation. For chambers and any other pressure equipment: the manufacturer's data report, the registration number, and the most recent inspection records.
  • Drawings and manuals. Electrical schematics and operating manuals. Without them, a field evaluation or engineering review gets slower and more expensive.
  • Change history. What's been repaired, rewired or modified, and by whom.
  • Your own power supply. Does the machine's voltage and phase match your building's service? Converting either is a cost of its own.
  • Parts and consumables. Can you still get envelopes, rims, rasp blades and spare parts that fit? Equipment you can't keep supplied isn't cheap for long.
  • Safety review. Ontario's pre-start health and safety review rules can apply when equipment is installed or modified. See our workplace safety guide.

Used, new, or a mix

None of this means used equipment is a bad idea. A used machine with a recognized mark, a valid Ontario registration and full documentation can be a sound way to lower the cost of a new line. What changes the math is the machine that arrives without paperwork. The cost of approvals, engineering and downtime while you wait comes on top of the purchase price, and it can be hard to estimate until someone has looked at the unit.

Whichever way you go, ask the same question of every seller and every supplier, including us: what is the approval path for this equipment in Ontario, and what documentation comes with it? Get the answer in writing before you order. Then put the full installed cost of each option into your startup budget.
