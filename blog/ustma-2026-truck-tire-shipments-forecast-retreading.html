Replacement Truck Tire Shipments Forecast to Drop 7.1% in 2026: What It Means for Casings and Retreading

USTMA's latest forecast cut 2026 U.S. replacement truck tire shipments to 22.9 million units, down 7.1%, while original-equipment truck tires rise. What the numbers say, what they don't, and what they mean for fleets running or planning a retread program.

Published November 27, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

Every year the U.S. Tire Manufacturers Association (USTMA) publishes a forecast of how many tires its members and the wider industry expect to ship into the U.S. market. Its latest update for 2026, released in August, made one segment stand out: replacement truck tires. USTMA now expects them to fall 7.1% this year, the largest downward revision of any category, while truck tires shipped on new vehicles go up.

Forecasts like this are easy to over-read. Here is what the numbers say, what they don't, and what they mean for a fleet that retreads its tires, or is thinking about bringing retreading in-house.

What USTMA forecast

USTMA's July 2026 forecast, published August 6, 2026, projects:

  • Total U.S. tire shipments of 330.3 million units in 2026, down 1.8% from 336.3 million in 2025.
  • Replacement truck tires: 22.9 million units, down 7.1% from 24.7 million in 2025. Even so, that's still 21.1% above the 18.9 million units shipped in 2019.
  • Original-equipment (OE) truck tires: 4.6 million units, up 6.2% from 4.4 million in 2025, though slightly below USTMA's earlier projection of 4.7 million.

The direction changed quickly. USTMA's February 2026 forecast expected a record 338.9 million total shipments and roughly flat replacement truck shipments of 24.7 million. By August it had cut the total by 8.6 million units, and replacement truck tires took the biggest cut.

What the numbers don't tell you

Shipments are tires sold into the market by manufacturers. They aren't the same as tires fitted to trucks or worn out on the road. We didn't find a stated reason from USTMA for the truck replacement cut, so the explanations below are other people's readings, not USTMA's.

  • Freight is flat, not collapsing. Industry analysis firm Radial Insights notes that ATA's truck tonnage index was up 1.4% year to date in July 2026, and argues that the drop points more toward channel and import timing than to trucks wearing out fewer tires.
  • Tariffs and inventory. Michelin's 2026 commentary, as reported by Tire Review, describes tariffs in 2025 followed by slower imports and distributors working down inventory in 2026. When dealers buy ahead of a tariff and then sell from stock, shipments fall even if tires keep wearing at the same rate.
  • No import breakdown. Klean Industries points out that USTMA's published table doesn't separate imported from domestic tires, so the import explanation can't be tested from the forecast alone.

In short, a shipment forecast tells you about the new-tire supply chain. It doesn't tell you a fleet is suddenly using fewer tires per mile.

Is there a Canadian number?

We didn't find an equivalent 2026 Canadian truck tire shipment forecast from the Tire and Rubber Association of Canada. The U.S. figures are a useful signal for a market that crosses the border every day, but they aren't Canadian numbers, and we haven't treated them as such.

Canada has its own source of uncertainty in the new-tire market right now. The Canada Border Services Agency opened dumping and subsidy investigations into truck and bus tires from China on August 31, 2026, and its initiation notice puts the preliminary determination, the first point at which provisional duties could apply, at November 30, 2026. Our earlier guide explains how that process works. We'll update it once CBSA's preliminary decision is published.

What it means for a retread program

The points below are our reading of the data above. They aren't forecasts of our own.

Tire wear follows miles, not shipments

A dip in replacement shipments doesn't change how fast tread wears on a loaded trailer. Retread demand comes from casings reaching their pull point, and that is driven by miles and maintenance, not by how many new tires dealers ordered this quarter. A fleet's own tire records are a better planning tool than any national forecast. Our guide to tire pull points covers how to set them so casings stay retreadable.

More OE tires now means more first-life casings later

OE truck tire shipments are forecast to rise 6.2%. Every new truck or trailer that goes into service brings a full set of new casings with it. For a fleet that retreads, those first-life casings are the best feedstock it will get. Whether the fleet captures that value depends on its casing policy: pulling tires before the casing is damaged, keeping them when the truck is traded, and tracking them. See casing ownership and RFID casing tracking.

Retreading reduces exposure to new-tire market swings

In just over a year the new truck tire market has seen tariffs, an import slowdown, distributor destocking, and in Canada an anti-dumping case. A fleet that gets more lives from each casing buys fewer new tires, so each of those swings hits fewer of its purchases. We've covered the cost side of that in rubber, oil and energy costs, the Section 232 tariff and ATRI's 2026 cost-per-mile data.

That case is strongest when you control the line

Buying fewer new tires only reduces your exposure to supply swings if your retread supply is steady too. A fleet that relies on a single outside retreader is still exposed to that retreader's capacity, pricing and turnaround. One that runs its own line decides its own production schedule and can buy tread rubber and consumables from more than one source. Our posts on single-supplier lock-in and in-house retreading ROI set out when the volume justifies that step.

Don't plan around a single forecast

USTMA moved its 2026 total by 8.6 million units in about five months. That's normal for forecasts, but it's a good reason to build tire budgets on your own fleet data and treat national forecasts as context.
