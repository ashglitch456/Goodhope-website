IN-HOUSE RETREADING

Where an In-House Retread Program Starts: Trailer and Drive Positions First

Rules, EPA's SmartWay program and common fleet practice all point retreads at trailer and drive axles. Why that makes them the sensible place to start a fleet's own retread line, and how to phase in from there.

Published November 12, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

A fleet that decides to retread its own tires doesn't have to retread every tire on day one. Most fleets already use retreads somewhere, and where they use them is fairly settled: on trailer and drive axles. Regulations, EPA's SmartWay program and long-standing fleet practice all point the same way. For a fleet bringing retreading in-house, those positions are the natural place to start, then grow from there.

This guide covers what the rules and published guidance say about retreads by axle position, and how a fleet can use that to phase in its own line. Sourcing note: we read the regulations and trade coverage through search extracts. Where we give our own reasoning rather than a published finding, we say so.

What the rules say about position

  • U.S. federal rule. Under 49 CFR 393.75(d), no bus may be operated with regrooved, recapped or retreaded tires on its front wheels. Trucks and truck tractors are not barred from retreads on the front. The truck restriction covers regrooved tires above a set load capacity, not retreads. FMCSA's own guidance says the only commercial motor vehicle that may not use retreaded tires is a bus, and only on its front wheels.
  • Practice on steer axles. Even where retreads are allowed on a truck's steer axle, trade coverage notes that many fleets keep them off it by choice, not because a regulation requires it.
  • Canada. Steer-axle rules for retreads are set differently in Canada. Check the current rules for your province and vehicle type. Our steer-axle rules guide compares the two countries.

EPA's SmartWay program treats retreads as drive and trailer tires

EPA's SmartWay program verifies low rolling resistance new and retread tires. Its guidance (page last updated January 2026) says retread technologies can only be used on two axle positions: trailer and drive. Fleets running multi-position tires are told to confirm with the tire maker that a tire suits the intended application. EPA keeps the list of verified retread designs current on its site, and trade coverage advises checking EPA directly because the list changes. More detail is in our SmartWay retreads guide.

What fleets already do

  • Retreads across trailer and drive positions. Transport Topics described one large fleet that puts retreads on all drive and all trailer tires once the original tread wears to its replacement limit. The same article reports that fleets spend 30% to 40% less on retreads than on new tires on average.
  • Casings move between positions. Heavy Duty Trucking's coverage of trailer tire strategy notes that operators often retread used drive tires for free-rolling trailer positions, and retread good trailer casings for drive service.
  • Trailer tires have their own wear. The same coverage points out that trailer tires take tight-turn scrub as the trailer pivots through corners, and that trailer axles tend to get alignment and attention less often than tractor axles. TMC's Radial Tire Conditions Analysis Guide breaks wear conditions down by steer, drive and trailer position, and for several trailer wear conditions its recommended disposition is to run to the pull point and then retread.

Why that makes trailer and drive the place to start

This section is our reasoning, built on the facts above. It is not a published finding.

  • Volume is where retreads already go. A tractor-trailer combination has far more tires on drive and trailer axles than on the steer axle, and those are the positions where fleets already accept retreads. An in-house line sized for those positions has steady work from the start. Our line-sizing guide covers matching capacity to volume.
  • Fewer tread designs to stock. A program limited to trailer and drive positions can start with a short list of tread designs and sizes, which keeps tread rubber inventory and curing setups simple. See matching tread designs to position.
  • The team learns on familiar ground. Inspection, buffing, building and curing are the same steps whatever the position. Starting on positions where the fleet already runs retreads lets technicians build skill and records before the program grows.
  • The fleet's own casings feed it. Drive and trailer tires coming off the fleet at the pull point become the casing supply. The fleet decides when tires come off and how they are handled, which protects casing quality. See tire pull points and casing ownership.
  • The fleet sets the pace. With its own line, the fleet decides when to add sizes, positions or capacity, rather than working to an outside supplier's schedule and pricing.

A phased plan

  • Phase 1: trailer positions. Start with the fleet's most common trailer size and one or two tread designs. Track casing salvage rate and turnaround from day one (see casing salvage rate).
  • Phase 2: drive positions. Add drive tread designs once inspection and cure quality are consistent. Use the casing records from Phase 1 to decide which casings go to drive service.
  • Phase 3: expand by data. Add sizes, medium-duty casings or other vehicle types based on what the numbers show. Any decision on steer-axle retreads follows the fleet's own policy and the rules for its jurisdiction and vehicle type.

The equipment list is the same in every phase: inspection, buffing, skiving and repair, building, enveloping and curing. What grows is the throughput. Our setup cost guide and ROI guide cover the investment side.
