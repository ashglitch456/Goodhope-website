Retread Failure Analysis: Tracing a Returned Tire Back to the Step That Caused It

How to read a failed retread: why most failures start outside the shop, which conditions do point back to the retread process, and how to map porosity, separations and splice problems to buffing, materials, building and curing.

Published November 23, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

Every retread that comes back early carries information. It can tell you whether a casing should have been rejected, whether a buffing or cure step drifted, or whether the tire simply hit something on the road. When a fleet sends its casings to an outside retreader, that information mostly stays with the retreader. When the fleet runs its own line, failed tires come back to the people who built them, and reading them properly is one of the main ways a line improves.

This guide covers how to approach a returned retread: what the industry's standard failure-analysis reference covers, why you should rule out causes outside the shop first, and how to trace the conditions that do point back to the line to a specific step.

The reference most fleets use: TMC's Radial Tire Conditions Analysis Guide

The Technology & Maintenance Council (TMC) of the American Trucking Associations publishes the Radial Tire Conditions Analysis Guide as a recommended practice. TMC's 2019 index of recommended practices lists it as RP 216, and when the Tire Retread & Repair Information Bureau (TRIB) announced the fourth edition in 2014 it described more than 200 colour photographs and illustrations covering failure and service conditions for both original and retreaded tires. Editions change, so buy the current one from TMC rather than relying on old copies circulating online.

The guide has a section for retread and repair conditions, which concentrate in the crown area of the tire. It groups them by what you see, including:

  • Holes and injuries, such as bad spot repairs and bias-type repairs in radial tires.
  • Missing or loose tread, including bond line porosity, tread separation and tread chunking at the splice.

For each condition the guide sets out the appearance, the probable cause and the recommended action. That three-part format is a good model for your own returned-tire log.

Step one: look at the whole tire before blaming the retread

The TMC guide warns that you should inspect the complete tire before deciding what caused the failure, because a separation is often a symptom of a problem somewhere else in the tire. That's the right starting point, because most tire failures don't start in the retread process.

  • Road hazards lead. In NHTSA's Commercial Medium Truck Tire Debris Study (December 2008), road hazard was the most common cause among fragments and casings whose retread status was known, at 38% and 36% respectively. The study also found that the share of debris from retreads was similar to the share of retreads in service.
  • Underinflation and overload come next. The same study concluded that keeping correct air pressure is key to tire performance, safety and durability. Tire industry guidance warns that underinflation or overloading causes excessive heat build-up and internal structural damage that can lead to tread or belt separation, sometimes well after the damage occurred. (We cover the pressure side in Underinflation and Your Casings.)
  • True adhesion failures are uncommon. A forensic engineering firm that analyses tire failures notes that the common perception that retreads lose their tread through adhesion problems doesn't match reality: that type of failure is very uncommon.

So before anyone opens a ticket against the line, check the casing for a penetration or impact, look for signs of run-flat or low-pressure operation, and pull the vehicle's pressure and inspection records if you have them. A tire that ran underinflated for weeks will separate regardless of how well it was built.

Step two: when the evidence points back to the line

Some conditions do implicate the retread process, and the TMC guide is specific about them. Its entry for bond line porosity describes large sections, or all, of the tread separated from the casing with porosity visible. It lists the probable causes as faulty retread workmanship or materials, such as:

  • a scorched or contaminated buffed surface,
  • old cushion gum or tread rubber,
  • improper cure conditions,
  • a missed nail hole, or
  • a faulty repair.

The guide's glossary defines porosity as a rubber condition with many small pores, usually the result of a lack of heat or pressure during curing. Every item on that list maps to a step on your own line, which is what makes the list useful:

  • Scorched buffed surface → buffing. Check rasp condition and selection, feed rate and whether the buffer is overheating the surface (see Rasp Blade Selection and Buffing and Rasping for Bond Quality).
  • Contaminated buffed surface → handling between buffing and building. Look at buffing dust control, how long buffed casings wait before building, and whether hands, gloves or the shop air are introducing oil or moisture.
  • Old cushion gum or tread rubber → stock rotation. Check the lot and age of the materials used on that tire against your first-in, first-out records (see Tread Rubber and Cushion Gum Storage).
  • Improper cure conditions → curing. Pull the cure record for that load: temperature, pressure and time. Check the envelope for leaks and the chamber for pressure loss (see Curing Envelopes and Vacuum Integrity).
  • Missed nail hole or faulty repair → inspection and repair. Go back to the initial inspection and the repair record for that casing (see The 5-Point Casing Check and Skiving and Casing Repair).

The TMC guide's recommended action for bond line porosity is to consult the retreader about a possible warranty adjustment. On your own line, the retreader is you. That conversation becomes a root-cause review, and it ties directly into how you run your warranty and adjustment policy.

Splices, undertread and crown shape

Two other sources of trouble are worth checking whenever a retread fails in the crown.

  • The tread splice. TMC lists tread chunking at the splice among its retread conditions, and forensic examiners note that a casing can show whether a failure started at the retread splice. Patent literature on retread splicing points out that the contact area between the two bonded tread ends is one factor in how well the tread stays attached in service. Follow your tread supplier's splicing procedure exactly, and record which builder made each tire so splice problems can be traced to a person or a habit rather than guessed at.
  • Undertread and crown profile. The Tire Industry Association's recommended practice RP-01-01, Tire Profiling for Retreading (updated 11/2019), warns that improper buffing can leave insufficient undertread and an improper tread crown, which can cause crooked-tread problems, and that retreads with excessive undertread may run hotter. If returned tires show uneven or off-centre wear, check buffed radius and width against the tread specification before blaming the tread rubber.

Step three: build the feedback loop

Failure analysis only pays off if the findings change something on the line. A simple system for an in-house shop:

  • Tag and hold every early return. Don't scrap a failed retread until someone has looked at it. Photograph the condition and the casing interior.
  • Log it in the TMC format. Record appearance, probable cause and action, plus the casing ID, retread date, builder, material lots and cure load. A casing tracking system makes that history quick to pull up.
  • Classify the cause. Split returns into outside causes (road hazard, pressure, mechanical) and process causes (buffing, materials, building, curing, inspection and repair). The outside causes go back to fleet maintenance. The process causes go to the line.
  • Look for clusters. One porosity failure might be a fluke. Three from the same cure load, builder or rubber lot is a process problem.
  • Close the loop at final inspection. Once you know what failed in the field, add it to what your inspectors look for before a tire leaves the shop (see Final Inspection After Curing).

Why this matters more when you run the line

An outside retreader can tell a fleet that a failure was caused by road hazard or underinflation, and the fleet usually has to take that on trust. When you run your own line, you see the evidence yourself, you decide what counts as a process failure, and you fix the step that caused it. Over time, that's how an in-house program improves its casing salvage rate and keeps its cost per retread down.
