Exposed Bead Wire or Cord: The FMVSS 117 Hard Stop on Casing Acceptance

Under FMVSS 117, a casing with exposed bead wire or cord fabric before processing cannot be retreaded, and repairs do not change that. What the standard and NHTSA say, and how an in-house line can screen for it at intake.

Published October 22, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

Most casing rejects are judgement calls: how big is the injury, how old is the casing, is the belt package sound. One rejection is not. In the US retread standard, a casing with exposed bead wire or exposed cord fabric before processing is out, and no repair changes that. For a fleet that runs its own line, that makes the bead area one of the cheapest places to catch an unretreadable casing before any rubber is removed.

This guide summarises the wording of the standard and two NHTSA interpretation letters, then suggests how an intake inspection can use them. It is deliberately short and carries no cost figures, because none of the sources we reached measured one.

A note on sourcing

We could not open the primary pages (eCFR, NHTSA) from our research environment, so what follows reflects what search results reported from each publisher. Read the current text of 49 CFR 571.117 and the NHTSA letters yourself before you build a policy on them. This post covers the US federal standard only; we did not research Canadian or Ontario requirements for this post, so confirm what applies to your operation.

What FMVSS 117 says

Standard No. 117 (Retreaded Pneumatic Tires), codified at 49 CFR 571.117, sets requirements at S5.2.1 for the casing. As reproduced in the search results from the eCFR/Cornell LII and J. J. Keller compliance texts, it reads that no retreaded tire shall be manufactured with a casing:

  • (a) on which bead wire or cord fabric is exposed before processing; or
  • (b) on which any cord fabric is exposed during processing, with an exception: cord fabric located at a splice, or cord fabric that is part of the belt material, may be exposed but must not be penetrated or removed to any extent whatsoever.

Two practical points follow from the wording. Condition (a) is about the casing as it arrives, before any buffing. Condition (b) is about what happens on your own line during processing, which is why buffing depth and rasp work matter (see buffing and rasping for bond quality).

What NHTSA has said about it

  • Repairs do not cure it. In a 1974 interpretation (reported as letter aiam1438), NHTSA said “processing” encompasses the entire process by which a casing is retreaded, including making any needed repairs. Its position, as summarised, is that a casing that before processing has any exposed bead or cord material other than chafer fabric may not be retreaded.
  • Chafer fabric is the one exception. The same letter says casings with exposed chafer fabric may be retreaded. Another interpretation (aiam0587) addressed cord exposed by chipping: if what is exposed is ply cord, S5.2.1 as written would preclude retreading, but if it is chafer, a special fabric placed only around the bead, the exposure is permitted.

Taken together: the question an inspector has to answer is not “is something showing?” but “what exactly is showing?” Bead wire or ply cord means reject; chafer fabric does not.

What an in-house line can do with this

This section is reasoning, not data. A fleet that runs its own line inspects the casing at intake, before it has spent anything on buffing, repair materials or curing. That is the cheapest moment to apply the S5.2.1(a) test:

  • Make the bead-area check a named step in your casing inspection, on both beads, before the casing moves on.
  • Train inspectors to tell chafer from ply cord. NHTSA treats them differently, so an inspector who cannot distinguish them will either scrap good casings or pass bad ones. Use a written reference and a sign-off from whoever owns your quality procedure.
  • Record the reject reason. Add “exposed bead or cord” as a coded reason in your casing record so you can see where in your fleet or supply of casings it shows up.
  • Do not repair your way past it. Per NHTSA’s reading, repairs are part of processing. A repair does not turn a casing with exposed bead or cord into an eligible one.
  • Keep the compliance picture whole. This is one requirement among several; see DOT certification and FMVSS 117 for an in-house line.

The point is control: when the casing decision is made at your own intake bench, against a written standard, it is yours to apply consistently and to audit. We are not claiming a specific saving from doing so.

What we are not claiming

  • We are not publishing a fleet example, a reject rate or a dollar figure. None of our sources measured one.
  • We are not giving legal advice or a compliance sign-off. Confirm the current regulation text, and any Canadian or provincial requirements, before setting policy.
  • We did not cover what causes bead damage; this post is about the acceptance rule, not its causes.
