The State of the Retread Industry in 2026: What TRIB's Survey Tells Fleets

TRIB's 2026 survey: 46% of North American retread businesses saw revenue fall in 2025, imports top the threat list, and 73% call cost savings extremely important to customers. Plus where the US retread bills stood after TRIB's November meeting.

Published November 18, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

Every spring the Tire Retread & Repair Information Bureau (TRIB) asks the North American retread business how it's doing. The 2026 answer, released May 11, was blunt about the year just gone and more hopeful about the year ahead. It also says a lot about what fleets actually want from retreads, and about where the people who make them are putting their money.

Here's what the survey found, what the trade press has added since, and where the proposed US retread tax credit stood after TRIB's annual meeting on November 2.

Who TRIB asked

This was TRIB's fourth annual State of the Retread Industry Survey. It covers the whole supply chain, not just retread plants. Respondents included retreaders, tread rubber manufacturers, materials and equipment suppliers, and casing and used-tire dealers across North America. TRIB's release was republished by the Tire and Rubber Association of Canada and covered by Retreading Business.

2025 was a hard year

The headline number: 46% of respondents reported lower revenue in 2025 than in 2024. Only 18% reported growth.

That matches what the trade press saw over the same stretch. FleetOwner described a market being reshaped by cost inflation and competition from imports. Modern Tire Dealer's 2026 Top 50 U.S. Retreaders report described 2025 as a year of higher operating costs, labour shortages and low-cost imported new tires. (MTD's ranking is based on tread rubber usage, so it compares output fairly across tire segments.)

The biggest threat, by a wide margin: cheap imported new tires

Respondents overwhelmingly named low-cost imported new tires as the industry's top competitive threat. TRIB has said it will use that finding to push for a legislative response.

The reason this matters for fleets is simple. Retreading depends on a casing that can take another tread. When a cheap new tire is priced close to a retread, some buyers will pick the new tire up front, even if it costs more per kilometre over its life. Canada has its own trade action on this front. We covered it in Canada's dumping investigation into Chinese truck tires.

The outlook for 2026 is better

TRIB managing director David Stevens said respondents' mood heading into 2026 was meaningfully more positive. MTD's Top 50 report agreed, describing an industry that entered 2026 on steadier footing after a tough 2025.

Where retreaders say they're investing

When TRIB asked where businesses plan to spend, four areas topped the list:

  • Workforce training
  • Software and IT systems
  • New retread equipment and plant expansion
  • Automation and robotics

Training at the top fits the labour shortage MTD described. We looked at what that shortage means for fleets in The Tire Technician Shortage. The equipment and automation items tell you something too. Even after a weak year, the industry is putting money into capacity and productivity, not pulling back.

What customers care about most: cost

The survey also asked what matters to retread customers. 73% of respondents rated cost savings "Extremely Important." Performance and product availability came close behind.

That's the fleet's view, as seen by the people selling to fleets. Retreads win on cost per kilometre, and they keep winning only if they perform and are available when the fleet needs them. For a fleet buying retreads from someone else, availability depends on that supplier's capacity and schedule. For a fleet running its own line, it depends on the fleet's own planning.

Where the US retread tax credit stands

The legislative push TRIB mentioned centres on two US bills: H.R. 3401, the Retreaded Tire Jobs, Supply Chain Security and Sustainability Act of 2025, and its Senate companion, S. 2790. We explained them in detail in our September post. Here's the update.

  • What the credit would be: the lesser of 30% of the purchase price or $30 per qualified retreaded tire, per the introduced bill text on Congress.gov. No credit applies to tires placed in service after December 31, 2028.
  • Federal purchasing: the bill would also require federal agencies to buy retreads from the GSA schedule when one is available in the needed size, load range and tread design.
  • Status: both bills are still at the introduced stage. H.R. 3401 was referred to the House Ways and Means and Oversight committees in May 2025. S. 2790 went to the Senate Finance Committee in September 2025.
  • Support: at TRIB's annual meeting in Las Vegas on November 2, Stevens said TRIB and the Tire Industry Association remain committed to both bills. MTD and Traction News reported that support stood at 10 House members and three senators, with no new backers since August.
  • Next steps: advocates are looking at attaching the measure to a larger package, such as year-end tax legislation, defense authorization or surface transportation reauthorization.

One note for Canadian fleets: this is a proposed US federal tax measure. As written, it isn't a Canadian incentive. It's worth watching mainly as a signal of political support for retreading, and for its possible effect on the cross-border market.

What this means for a fleet

Put the survey together and you get a clear picture. The industry had a tough 2025, is investing in training, equipment and automation, and sees cheap imported new tires as its biggest threat. Its customers care most about cost, then performance and availability.

The environmental side hasn't changed either. The US Tire Manufacturers Association's 2026 retreading fact sheet credits each retread with about 15 gallons less oil and 90 pounds less material than a new tire.

For fleets running enough tires, the survey points to a practical question. If cost, performance and availability are what you care about, how much control do you have over each? A fleet that buys all its retreads from one outside supplier depends on that supplier's pricing, capacity and turnaround. A fleet with its own line controls all three, along with the casings it owns.
