Casing Tracking on an In-House Retread Line: Barcode, RFID and the Records That Matter

Every casing has a history that decides whether it can be retreaded again. What the trade press says about barcode and RFID tracking, and what a fleet running its own line should record.

Published October 20, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

A casing is only worth what you can prove about it. Its age, the positions it ran in, the repairs it has taken and how many times it has already been retreaded all decide whether it goes back on the line or out the door as scrap. If that history lives in a driver’s memory or on a paint mark that the buffer removes, every casing decision becomes a guess. Tracking is the fix, and a fleet running its own line is in a good position to do it properly.

This guide covers what the trade press says about tracking, the barcode-versus-RFID debate, and a practical list of what to record. It is deliberately light on numbers: the sources we could reach this run describe practices, not measured savings, so we are not publishing a savings figure.

What the published material says

The figures and descriptions below come from trade-press and industry pages surfaced in our research. We could not open the primary pages directly from our research environment, so each item reflects what the search results reported and should be checked against the original before you rely on it.

  • Barcode per casing. A major tread-rubber manufacturer’s retreading guidance and a Heavy Duty Trucking photo gallery both describe branded-network retreaders assigning a unique barcode to every casing so it can be followed in real time through the process.
  • Collection to delivery. Tire Technology International reported on a casing tracking and management platform that covers the whole loop from collecting casings at the fleet through to delivering the retreaded tire. It describes dealer-side production tracking by scan and fleet-side reporting such as acceptance rates and the number of lifecycles per casing. A 2026 Retreading Business article describes the same platform integrating with ERP systems.
  • Labels that survive buffing. Fleet Equipment magazine covered a permanent tire bar code product whose label information is cut into the rubber so that it can survive the retread process.
  • Casing analytics is a stated industry focus. A press release from the top-ranked retreader in Modern Tire Dealer’s 2026 Top 50 U.S. Retreaders says the report emphasizes the growing importance of casing analytics and performance tracking. That is the company’s framing of the report, so treat it as such. The report itself, as described in the same coverage, also cites labor constraints and volatile casing availability and pricing.

Barcode or RFID? The trade press is not settled

Retreading Business has argued that RFID tags add to production cost and can fail during the life of the tire, and it has promoted a scanning-based alternative. A retreading-materials supplier takes the opposite view for its RFID patch, which is designed to be vulcanized into the casing so the record survives when a paper label is lost. FleetOwner notes that RFID adds readers and infrastructure on top of the tags, which can put it out of reach for smaller fleets.

Each of those positions comes from a party with something to sell, so we are not picking a winner. The practical point for a fleet is narrower: whichever identifier you choose has to survive buffing and still be readable on the next cycle, and your process has to re-identify the casing each time it goes through the line. One retread-shop account we found says a casing gets a new barcode for each retread.

What an in-house line can record

This section is reasoning, not data. A fleet that retreads its own casings sees them at every stage, from removal at the yard to the finished tire back on a wheel position. That means it can build the history that an outside program would otherwise hold. A sensible minimum record for each casing:

  • Identity and age. A unique ID plus the manufacturer’s date code from the sidewall. Casing age policy is set from that code, so it should be captured at intake (see casing age limits).
  • Retread generation. How many times the casing has been through the line.
  • Repairs. Type, location and date of each repair, matched to your repair limits.
  • Wheel positions and removal reason. Where it ran, and why it came off (worn out, damage, pulled early).
  • Inspection result. Accepted or rejected at intake inspection, with the reason when rejected.
  • Finished measurements. Buffed and finished diameter, which feeds dual matching.

Once those fields exist, the questions that matter can be answered from your own data: which casing brands survive a second life in your operation, which routes or positions chew casings fastest, and what your casing salvage rate actually is. We are not quoting an industry-average acceptance rate here because we could not find one from a primary industry source this run.

A regulatory note on tire identification

Separately from your own casing records, the federal tire identification rules are in 49 CFR Part 574. Per the current eCFR text as reported in our research, a tire retreader must label at least one sidewall of each tire it retreads with a seven-symbol tire identification number. The same search results indicated that the part does not apply to persons who retread tires solely for their own use. We could not confirm that exemption against the primary text or how NHTSA applies it to a fleet line, and Canadian requirements are a separate question. Read our guide to DOT certification for an in-house line, and confirm your obligations directly with the regulator before you decide how to mark finished tires.

What we are not claiming

  • We are not publishing a fleet example or a dollar figure for tracking. The sources describe practices and vendor positions, not measured savings.
  • Vendor claims about acceptance-rate improvements or return on investment from tracking software are not repeated here, because we could not tie them to an independent source.
  • Tracking does not make a line pay on its own. It is one of several controls that give a fleet visibility into casing value, alongside casing ownership.
