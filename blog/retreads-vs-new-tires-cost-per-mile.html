Retreads vs. New Tires: What the Cost-Per-Mile Numbers Actually Show

Real cost-per-mile and safety data — from UMTRI/NHTSA, TIA, and TRIB — not the recycled "retreads are cheaper" line you've heard a hundred times.

Published August 12, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

Every fleet manager has heard the pitch: retreads are cheaper than new tires. What's harder to find is the actual math, and the safety data that answers the objection that usually kills the conversation before it gets there. This isn't another post repeating an industry rule of thumb — every figure below traces to a named source: research the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) commissioned from the University of Michigan Transportation Research Institute (UMTRI), and published positions from the Tire Industry Association (TIA) and the Tire Retread & Repair Information Bureau (TRIB).

If you run a retread shop, treat this as a resource to hand a fleet customer who's still on the fence — real citations, not sales copy.

The cost-per-mile math

The core economic case for retreading hasn't changed: you're paying for a new tread on a casing you already own, instead of an entirely new tire — casing, tread, and all. Trade-press and fleet-cost reporting consistently puts retread cost-per-mile at roughly 30–50% below a comparable new tire, depending on tire class and application. A well-maintained steer, drive, or trailer casing can typically go through two to three retread cycles before it's retired, so the savings compound — closer to three tire "lives" for a fraction of the cost of three new tires.

At the fleet level that adds up fast. TIA and TRIB estimate that retreading saves the North American trucking industry on the order of $3 billion a year in tire costs — money that comes straight off cost-per-mile, the number every fleet is under pressure to bring down as diesel and equipment costs climb through 2026.

The safety objection — and what the data actually says

The most common reason fleets hesitate on retreads isn't cost — it's the assumption that retreads are more likely to fail on the highway, pointing to the tire debris ("road gators") you see on the shoulder. It's a persistent myth, and it's aimed at the wrong cause.

NHTSA commissioned UMTRI to settle the question directly with its Commercial Medium Tire Debris Study. Researchers collected roughly 86,000 pounds of tire and casing debris from highways across five states — Arizona, California, Florida, Indiana, and Virginia — and traced its origin. Of the debris that could be identified, 78% came from new tires and only 22% came from retreads. New tires, not retreads, are the larger source of road debris — a finding widely reported at the time, including by Tire Business.

What actually causes tire failure

The same UMTRI research looked at why casings were pulled from service in the first place, across both new and retreaded tires. The leading causes were road hazards (32%) and maintenance or operational factors like underinflation (30%), with over-deflected operation adding another 14%. Those causes hit new and retreaded tires in roughly equal measure — construction and maintenance quality drive failure, not whether a casing has been retreaded.

Separate survey data collected by the Technology & Maintenance Council (TMC) found retread tires failing due to a repair-related defect at 9% and 3% in its two survey years — 1 to 4 percentage points lower than the equivalent failure category for new tires. That lines up with the position TRIB and TIA both publish: a retread built to industry standard by a certified retreader performs comparably to a new tire. What actually disqualifies a tire is a damaged or poorly maintained casing — the same thing that eventually takes a new tire out of service too.

Why cost-per-mile discipline matters more in 2026

Commercial truck tire makers are describing 2026 as a market defined by cost pressure and cautious fleets, with buyers shifting focus from sticker price to total cost of ownership — durability, fuel efficiency, and retreadability — according to trade coverage from Modern Tire Dealer and Fleet Maintenance. Retreaders are responding with more casing analytics and program-based fleet partnerships rather than one-off sales, precisely because cost-per-mile — not per-tire sticker price — is what's actually being managed.

The bottom line

The "retreads are cheaper" pitch is true, but it isn't the strongest argument on the table — the strongest argument is that the safety case against retreading doesn't hold up against the data NHTSA itself commissioned. A properly built, properly inspected retread on a sound casing performs like a new tire, at a meaningfully lower cost-per-mile, with two or three lives out of one casing instead of one.

If you're running a retread line — or building one — the shop's own process discipline is what makes that case hold: sound casing inspection, a properly controlled cure, and consistent build quality.
