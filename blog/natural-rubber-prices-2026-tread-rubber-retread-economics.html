Natural Rubber Hit a 13-Year High This Fall: What It Means for Tread Rubber and Retread Economics

Rubber futures reached their highest level since 2013 in October 2026 as Thai flooding hit a market already short of supply. What the producer data shows, why truck tires are exposed, and what retreading changes.

Published November 14, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

Natural rubber is one of the main raw materials in a truck tire, and its price had a volatile autumn. In mid-October 2026, rubber futures climbed to their highest level since early 2013, driven by flooding in Thailand, the world's largest producer. Prices eased into November, but the supply picture behind the spike hasn't gone away.

This post covers what happened, what the producer data says about 2026, and why it matters to a fleet comparing new tires with retreads, or running its own retread line. Sourcing note: we read market reports and industry data through search extracts, and we list each source at the end. Prices move daily. This is a snapshot as of mid-November 2026, not a forecast.

What happened to rubber prices this fall

  • A 13-year high in October. Trading Economics reported that rubber futures reached about 265 US cents per kilogram in mid-October 2026, the highest since early 2013. It linked the move to flooding in Thailand that disrupted output.
  • Still elevated in early November. A November 2 Trading Economics update put futures at about 255 US cents per kilogram, not far from the October peak.
  • Some easing since. By November 11, Trading Economics described futures as hovering at a seven-week low. The same report said heavy rainfall in southern Thailand was adding to concerns about disruptions to tapping.
  • Other supply pressures. Market reports also cited El Niño-related weather affecting production and raw-material shortages in Indonesia. Higher oil prices limited the downside, because they raise the cost of petroleum-based synthetic rubber and make natural rubber relatively more competitive.

The supply picture: demand still ahead of production

  • A projected 2026 shortfall. The Association of Natural Rubber Producing Countries (ANRPC) has consistently projected that world demand for natural rubber will exceed production in 2026. In its August report, released in late September, ANRPC projected production rising 0.6% to 15.039 million tonnes and demand rising 0.4% to 15.356 million tonnes. That implies a shortfall of roughly 317,000 tonnes.
  • Weaker output so far this year. Reuters, citing ANRPC data, reported that production fell 4.2% year on year to 8.68 million tonnes in the first eight months of 2026, while consumption fell 2.7% to 9.89 million tonnes.
  • The forecasts move. ANRPC revised its numbers several times during the year, and other estimates of the gap differ. The direction has been consistent; the exact size of the shortfall has not.

A regulatory factor to watch: the EU deforestation rule

The EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR) covers seven commodities, including rubber. After a further one-year delay adopted in December 2025, it now applies from December 30, 2026, with micro and small operators given until June 30, 2027. It requires companies placing covered products on the EU market to show they are deforestation-free. Canadian fleets aren't directly regulated by it, but it adds a compliance requirement to the global rubber supply chain at a time when supply is already tight. We aren't predicting a price effect; it's simply one more factor in the market.

Why truck tires are especially exposed

  • Truck tires lean on natural rubber. Continental's truck tire whitepaper puts natural rubber at around 30% of the tire's compound, against around 5% synthetic rubber. A Continental spokesperson quoted in Transport Topics put natural rubber at about 35% of the weight of a truck tire's tread.
  • It's there for a reason. A tire designer quoted in Heavy Duty Trucking said commercial truck tire makers primarily use natural rubber for its heat resistance and durability, while car tires generally contain more synthetic rubber. That makes it hard to substitute when prices rise. Our tread compound basics post covers what else goes into a tread.

What retreading changes

A retread replaces only the worn tread. The casing, which holds most of the tire's material, goes back into service. The Tire Retread & Repair Information Bureau (TRIB) is widely cited in trade coverage as estimating that a new medium truck tire takes about 22 gallons of oil to make, against about 7 gallons for a retread, because the casing is reused.

The rest of this section is our reasoning, built on the facts above. It is not a published finding.

  • Less new material per tire life. Each retread adds new tread rubber, which contains natural rubber too, so retreads are not immune to rubber prices. But a retread uses far less new material than a new tire, so each tire life carries less raw-material exposure.
  • Casings become more valuable. When raw materials are expensive, a sound casing that can be retreaded again is worth protecting. Inflation maintenance, careful pull points and good inspection all matter more. See tire pull points and retread cycles per casing.
  • Don't stockpile past shelf life. When prices spike, it's tempting to buy tread rubber and cushion gum well ahead. Both have storage limits, so buy to a plan rather than a headline. Our guides on tread rubber storage and shelf life and consumables supply planning cover how much to hold.
  • Watch the whole tire market. Rubber is one input among many. New-tire prices in Canada are also shaped by trade measures, including the dumping and subsidy case on truck and bus tires from China, which we covered in our investigation explainer.
