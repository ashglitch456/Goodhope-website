Budgeting Labour for an In-House Retread Line in Ontario: What the Wage Data and Payroll Rules Say

How to build the labour line of an in-house retread budget in Ontario: Job Bank wage data for retreading and tire repair work, the 2026 payroll costs on top of wages, and how to turn it into a labour cost per retread using your own throughput.

Published November 23, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

Most in-house retreading budgets start with the equipment quote, because that's the biggest single number. Over the life of the line, though, labour is a recurring cost you pay every week, and it's one of the main things that decides your cost per retread. This guide sets out what public data says about wages for retreading work in Ontario, what an employer pays on top of those wages in 2026, and how to turn both into a labour cost per tire using your own numbers.

We won't give you a "typical" crew size or a minutes-per-tire figure. Those depend on your process, your equipment and your volume, and we haven't found a credible public source for them. What follows is a method, using real figures where they exist.

Start with the right occupation

Canada's Job Bank publishes wage data by occupation, and retreading work falls under two different codes, so it's worth knowing which is which:

  • Retreading production work sits under NOC 94112, rubber processing machine operators and related workers. Job Bank lists "tire-retreading machine tender" under this occupation, which covers people employed by tire manufacturers and other rubber products manufacturers.
  • Tire repair and service work sits under NOC 74203, which Job Bank shows as "tire repairer" or "tire technician" (other automotive mechanical installers and servicers).

A retread line's crew usually does a bit of both: inspection, buffing, repair, building, curing and final inspection. Use whichever code fits each role you're hiring for.

We couldn't find a separate figure for the Windsor–Sarnia region for either occupation, and some pages are a year or more old. Use the table as a starting range, then check current postings in your own area. Two other points stand out:

  • Retreading production pays more than basic tire service. The NOC 94112 median for Ontario is roughly $10 an hour above the tire repairer median. Budget for production wages if you want people who can run buffing, building and curing equipment well.
  • The legal floor keeps rising. Ontario's general minimum wage rose to $17.95 an hour on October 1, 2026, indexed to inflation each year. The low end of the tire repairer range is now close to that floor, which suggests entry-level pay will rise with it.

Finding people is a separate problem from paying them. We cover hiring and training in The Tire Technician Shortage.

What you pay on top of wages in 2026

The hourly wage isn't the full cost of an employee. For an Ontario employer in 2026, the main statutory items are:

  • Vacation pay: at least 4% of wages (two weeks) for most employees, rising to 6% (three weeks) after five years, under the Employment Standards Act, 2000.
  • Public holidays: Ontario has nine, and most employees are entitled to the day off with public holiday pay.
  • CPP: the employer matches the employee's 5.95% on earnings between the $3,500 basic exemption and the $74,600 ceiling, plus CPP2 at 4% on earnings from $74,600 to $85,000.
  • EI: the employer pays 1.4 times the employee rate of 1.63%, about 2.28%, on insurable earnings up to $68,900.
  • WSIB: the 2026 average premium is $1.23 per $100 of insurable earnings, and the WSIB has announced it will drop to $1.18 on January 1, 2027. That's only an average. Your actual rate depends on your business's classification, so get your own rate from the WSIB.
  • Employer Health Tax: eligible employers with Ontario payroll under $5 million are exempt on the first $1 million of payroll. Larger payrolls pay EHT at rates up to 1.95%.

Benefits, overtime, training time and protective equipment come on top of these, and they depend on your own policies.

Turning it into labour cost per retread

The formula is simple. The inputs have to come from your own line:

Labour cost per retread = (fully loaded hourly cost × crew hours per shift) ÷ retreads finished per shift

  • Retreads finished, not started. Count tires that pass final inspection. Casings rejected partway through still used labour, which is one reason casing salvage rate affects your cost per retread.
  • Size the crew to the bottleneck. In most lines the curing chamber sets the pace. Adding people upstream of a full chamber raises cost without raising output. Our line sizing guide covers matching capacity to volume.
  • Volume spreads fixed labour. A crew you pay for a full shift costs the same whether the chamber runs full or half empty. That's why the ROI case for in-house retreading depends so heavily on volume.
  • Cross-train. A small crew where each person can run more than one station is easier to keep busy than a crew of specialists who wait on each other.

Where labour fits in the in-house decision

When you buy retreads from an outside shop, its labour cost is built into the price per tire, along with its overhead and margin. When you run your own line, you see the labour cost directly, and you control it through staffing, scheduling and volume. The comparison that matters is your fully loaded cost per retread, including labour, materials, energy and equipment, against what you pay per retread today.
