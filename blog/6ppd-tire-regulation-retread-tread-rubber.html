6PPD in Tires: Where the Regulations Stand and What It Means for Retread Tread Rubber

6PPD protects tire rubber from ozone, and its by-product 6PPD-quinone is toxic to coho salmon. Here is where Canada, the U.S., California and the EU stand as of late 2026, why California's rule explicitly covers retreads, and what fleets and retread shops should watch.

Published November 26, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

Most fleet managers have never heard of 6PPD, yet it is in almost every tire they run. It is the chemical that keeps rubber from cracking in ozone. Over the past few years it has also become one of the most closely watched chemicals in the tire industry, because a compound it forms on the road, 6PPD-quinone, is toxic to coho salmon. Regulators in Canada, the U.S., California and the EU are all at different stages of deciding what to do about it.

For retreading, there is one detail worth knowing up front: California's rule explicitly covers retreaded tires, including the new tread. Here is where things stand as of November 2026, based on the regulators' own publications, and what it means for fleets and retread shops.

What 6PPD does, and why it's a problem

Ozone in the air attacks stretched rubber and causes cracking. 6PPD is an antidegradant that protects against it. One chemical supplier notes that tire makers began using 6PPD against oxygen, ozone and cracking in the 1960s, and that it is used in almost all commercial tires, especially in sidewalls and tread compounds. Researchers at UC Berkeley describe two protective actions: 6PPD consumes ozone at the tire surface before it can attack the rubber, and its reaction products then help shield the surface.

That same reaction is the problem. When 6PPD reacts with ozone it forms 6PPD-quinone (6PPD-Q), which reaches waterways in tire wear particles and road runoff. The Berkeley group notes that this mechanism ties 6PPD-Q's toxicity directly to the job 6PPD does in the tire. That is why finding a replacement is hard: anything that replaces it has to protect rubber just as well without forming a harmful by-product.

Where regulators stand

Canada: prioritized for assessment

On April 30, 2024, the federal government granted a request from environmental groups to prioritize 6PPD for assessment under the Canadian Environmental Protection Act. It was the first successful use of the new section 76 request process. Environment and Climate Change Canada and Health Canada's Plan of Priorities lists 6PPD and its transformation products, including 6PPD-Q, for assessment together. It notes that monitoring has detected 6PPD-Q in the Canadian aquatic environment at concentrations that may be of concern for coho salmon.

An assessment decides whether a substance is toxic under CEPA, and only then can regulation follow. As of this writing we found no published assessment conclusion and no Canadian rule restricting 6PPD in tires.

United States: an advance notice, but no proposed rule yet

In November 2023 the U.S. EPA granted a petition from three Pacific Northwest tribes under the Toxic Substances Control Act. In November 2024 it published an advance notice of proposed rulemaking on 6PPD and 6PPD-Q, asking for information on risks and on alternatives. We found no proposed rule since then. Separately, 6PPD and 6PPD-Q are among 16 chemicals covered by an EPA health and safety data reporting rule. In May 2026, EPA extended that reporting deadline to May 21, 2027.

There is also a lawsuit. Two commercial fishing organizations sued 13 tire manufacturers in federal court in California under the Endangered Species Act, seeking to stop the use of 6PPD without measures to protect fish. A bench trial began in January 2026. We have not found a published ruling.

California: the rule that names retreads

California's Department of Toxic Substances Control (DTSC) listed motor vehicle tires containing 6PPD as a Priority Product under its Safer Consumer Products program, effective October 1, 2023. Manufacturers had to notify DTSC and then carry out an alternatives analysis. Tires sold as part of a new vehicle and tires for exclusively off-road equipment are excluded.

For retreaders, the important part is the definition of "tire." DTSC's rulemaking documents bring retreaded tires into scope, covering the new tread material and the used components of a retreaded tire. DTSC also considered limiting the listing to tread alone and chose the whole tire instead, because exposure to 6PPD-Q can come from other parts of the tire too.

A tire manufacturers' consortium coordinated by the U.S. Tire Manufacturers Association received DTSC's notice of compliance for its first-stage report, which identified candidate alternatives. The second-stage report, with a deeper evaluation of hazards and exposure, was due August 19, 2026. We could not confirm DTSC's response to it at the time of writing.

European Union: a restriction in preparation

On March 26, 2026, the Netherlands and Austria notified the European Chemicals Agency that they intend to propose a REACH restriction on 6PPD and related para-phenylenediamines, using a group approach to avoid swapping one problem chemical for a close relative. A call for evidence ran from December 2025 to March 2026. The Norwegian Environment Agency reports that the restriction dossier is expected on March 12, 2027.

What it means for retreading

No Canadian rule changes what a fleet or retread shop must do today. But the direction is clear enough to plan around. The points below are our reading of the record above, not regulatory requirements:

  • Retreads aren't a loophole, and they aren't singled out. Tread wears the same way whether it was moulded on a new tire or applied in a retread shop. California's rule treats them alike, and any future rule elsewhere is likely to target the chemical rather than how the tire was made.
  • Tread rubber is where change would reach a retread shop. If antidegradant systems change, retreaders will see it first in tread rubber, cushion gum and precured tread specifications. Know what compounds you're running and ask suppliers for current technical and safety data sheets. Our primer on tread compound basics explains what goes into a compound.
  • Any new compound needs proper validation. 6PPD exists because ozone cracking is a real failure mode. A reformulated compound should be proven on ozone and flex-crack resistance, bond quality and wear before it goes on your fleet. Follow the material supplier's process specs for cure, storage and shelf life.
  • Casings sit in ozone too. Ozone protection matters while casings are stored before retreading. The TIA storage guidance in our casing storage guide already covers keeping rubber away from ozone sources like electric motors.
  • Retreading's sustainability case doesn't depend on this. The argument for retreading rests on reusing the casing, as our posts on retread carbon footprint data and scrap tire numbers show. We aren't claiming retreads reduce 6PPD-Q, and we haven't found evidence either way.

What to watch in 2027

  • A draft CEPA assessment of 6PPD from Environment and Climate Change Canada and Health Canada.
  • DTSC's response to the tire makers' second-stage alternatives analysis.
  • Any EPA proposed rule under TSCA, and the outcome of the Endangered Species Act case.
  • The EU restriction dossier, expected in March 2027.

We'll update our coverage as these land. In the meantime, the practical step for any shop is the same one that keeps quality consistent anyway: know your compounds, store materials and casings properly, and validate any material change before it reaches the road.
