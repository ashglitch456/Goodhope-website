Financing an In-House Retread Line in Canada: What the CSBFP and BDC Equipment Loans Offer

Equipment is the up-front hurdle for a fleet bringing retreading in-house. What the federal Canada Small Business Financing Program and BDC equipment loans offer in 2026, their limits, and how to fit them into the in-house decision.

Published November 14, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

The hardest part of bringing tire retreading in-house usually isn't the process. It's the up-front equipment purchase. Inspection, buffing, building and curing equipment all have to be paid for before the line saves anything. For Canadian fleets, two federal financing routes are worth knowing before you talk to a lender: the Canada Small Business Financing Program (CSBFP), which backs loans made by ordinary banks and credit unions, and the Business Development Bank of Canada (BDC), which lends directly.

This guide covers what each one offers for equipment, as of November 2026. Sourcing note: we read the government and BDC pages through search extracts, and we list each source at the end. This is general information, not financial or tax advice. Good Hope is an equipment supplier, not a lender or financing broker. Confirm current terms with your lender before you plan a purchase around them.

The Canada Small Business Financing Program (CSBFP)

The CSBFP is run by Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada (ISED). The government doesn't lend the money. A participating bank or credit union makes the loan and decides whether to approve it, and ISED registers the loan and pays the lender an eligible portion of its losses if the loan defaults. That backing is meant to make lenders more willing to finance small businesses.

  • Who qualifies. Start-ups and existing businesses in Canada with gross annual revenues of $10 million or less, according to ISED.
  • How much. Up to $1.15 million per borrower in total: up to $1 million in term loans plus up to $150,000 in lines of credit for working capital.
  • The equipment limit. Within the $1 million term-loan limit, no more than $500,000 can go to equipment and leasehold improvements combined. That cap was raised from $350,000 in the program changes that took effect on July 4, 2022. The Canada Gazette notice for those changes said the $350,000 limit had not moved since 2009, despite rising asset costs.
  • New or used equipment. Program guides list new or used equipment as eligible, with commercial vehicles among the examples. Lenders may ask for an appraisal on used equipment.
  • Term. Since July 4, 2022, term loans for equipment, leasehold improvements, real property, intangible assets and working capital can be made for a maximum 15-year term (the length of government coverage). Repayment can be amortized over a longer period.
  • Rate caps. Lenders set the rate, but ISED caps it. The maximum floating rate is the lender's prime rate plus 3%. The maximum fixed rate is the lender's posted single-family residential mortgage rate plus 3%. Both caps include a 1.25% annual administration fee. Lenders can charge less.
  • Registration fee. A one-time 2% fee, based on the amount loaned for a term loan or the amount authorized for a line of credit.

BDC equipment loans

  • What it covers. BDC's equipment loan page says it covers up to 125% of the purchase price of new or used equipment. A BDC article explains that the amount above the purchase price can cover costs such as shipping, installation and training.
  • Payment flexibility. The same BDC article notes that some lenders, including BDC, offer principal postponement for up to the first two years of an equipment loan. That can help while a new line is ramping up.
  • Not always zero down. BDC's page also notes that a down payment may be required in some cases. Treat the 125% figure as a maximum, not a promise.

Don't forget the tax side

Financing decides how you pay for the equipment. Capital cost allowance (CCA) decides how quickly you can deduct it. We covered the current CRA rules, including the Accelerated Investment Incentive and the newer immediate-expensing measures, in our CCA guide for in-house retread equipment. Bring both questions to your accountant together.

How this connects to the in-house decision

This section is our reasoning, built on the facts above. It is not a published finding.

  • Compare the loan payment to what you spend today. The useful question isn't "can we afford the equipment?" It's "how does the monthly loan payment, plus consumables and labour, compare with what we now pay an outside retreader?" Our ROI and payback guide walks through that comparison, and our setup cost guide covers what goes into the equipment list.
  • Match the term to the equipment. Curing chambers, buffers and builders are long-lived assets. A longer loan term lowers the monthly payment, but you pay interest for longer. Ask your lender to show both.
  • Size the line before you size the loan. The CSBFP's $500,000 equipment cap and your lender's limits are a ceiling, not a target. Buy for the volume your fleet actually generates. See our line sizing guide.
  • Plan for installation and training. If you use a loan that can cover more than the purchase price, ask your lender whether installation, freight and training qualify, and get them into the plan from the start.
  • Keep working capital for consumables. Tread rubber, cushion gum, cement and envelopes are an ongoing cost. Both programs offer working-capital options; see our consumables planning guide for what to budget.

What lenders will typically ask for

  • A written supplier quote for the equipment you plan to buy.
  • Financial statements and, for a new line, a business plan that shows how the equipment will be used and paid for.
  • Your own numbers: current tire spend, casings retreaded per month, and the expected volume on your own line. Pulling these together first makes the conversation faster.

Requirements vary by lender, so ask yours for the full list.
