Canada's China Truck Tire Dumping Case Clears Its First Hurdle: What the Tribunal Found and What Comes Next

On October 30, 2026, Canada's trade tribunal found a reasonable indication of injury from dumped and subsidized truck and bus tires from China, new or retreaded. What that means, the dates ahead, and what fleets should do now.

Published November 13, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

In September we wrote about Canada's new dumping and subsidy case on truck and bus tires from China. At that point the case was only an investigation. It has now passed its first checkpoint. On October 30, 2026, the Canadian International Trade Tribunal (CITT) found a reasonable indication that dumped and subsidized imports of these tires have injured the domestic industry. The case now moves to the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA), whose preliminary determinations are due by November 30, 2026.

Important upfront: no duties are in force today, and none are guaranteed. This post covers what the Tribunal decided, what happens next, and what it means for fleets and retread shops buying commercial tires in Canada. Sourcing note: we read the government notices and trade coverage through search extracts, and we name each source at the end. Where we give our own reasoning rather than a published finding, we say so.

What the Tribunal decided on October 30

  • The finding. In preliminary injury inquiry PI-2026-005, the Tribunal determined there is a reasonable indication that the dumping and subsidizing of truck and bus tires from China has caused injury to the domestic industry. This comes from the Tribunal's announcement as republished in news coverage.
  • What a "reasonable indication" means. It is a screening test, not a final ruling. Had the Tribunal found no reasonable indication, the case would have ended. Because it found one, the case continues.
  • Who brought the case. The complaint was filed by the Canadian Retread Manufacturers Association (CRMA) and Michelin North America (Canada) Inc. Secondary coverage reports that the complainants account for the majority of Canadian production of these tires.

What is in scope, including retreads

The product definition is the detail fleets should read closely. Per CBSA's case documents as summarized by trade-compliance firms, the case covers pneumatic tires, new or retreaded, for trucks, buses, trailers and other medium and heavy vehicles, in rim diameters of 17.5, 19.5, 22.5 and 24.5 inches. The goods are usually imported under tariff classification numbers 4011.20.00.13, 4011.20.00.19 and 4012.12.00.00.

So the case is not only about new tires. Imported retreaded truck and bus tires from China are also in scope. The parallel U.S. case on this product category covered new tires only, as we noted in our first post on this case.

The timeline from here

  • August 31, 2026. CBSA opened its dumping and subsidy investigations. (One CBSA document gives a different initiation date than the news release. The deadlines below are those CBSA and trade counsel have published.)
  • September 1, 2026. The Tribunal opened its preliminary injury inquiry.
  • October 30, 2026. The Tribunal found a reasonable indication of injury.
  • By November 30, 2026. CBSA's preliminary determinations of dumping and subsidizing are due. Under the Special Import Measures Act (SIMA), CBSA may apply provisional duties on imports from the date of an affirmative preliminary determination. CBSA's published process notes this deadline can be extended in some cases.
  • About 90 days after that. CBSA's final determination is due within 90 days of the preliminary determination, according to CBSA's published SIMA process.
  • About 120 days after the preliminary determination. If the case continues, the Tribunal runs a final injury inquiry and has 120 days from CBSA's preliminary determination to issue its finding.
  • If the case ends early. If CBSA terminates its investigation or the Tribunal finds no injury, the proceedings end and provisional duties are returned.

So the earliest point landed costs could change is the CBSA preliminary determination at the end of November, and only on a provisional basis. A final outcome is still months away.

What it means for fleets and retread shops

This section is our reasoning, built on the facts above. It is not a published finding.

  • Pricing on in-scope tires is unsettled. If provisional duties are applied, importers of in-scope tires may pay them from the preliminary determination onward. Fleets that buy imported new or retreaded truck tires should ask their suppliers now how a provisional duty would be handled in their pricing.
  • The case is about price, not tire quality. A dumping finding is about selling below fair value or with subsidies. It says nothing about how a tire performs. Fleets should keep choosing tires on cost per mile and casing quality, not on headlines.
  • A fleet's own casings are outside the import question. A fleet that retreads its own casings in Canada is not importing a finished tire. The duty question applies to imported in-scope tires, not to work done on casings the fleet already owns. Tread rubber and other retread materials are not part of the product definition summarized above, but any fleet importing materials should confirm scope with its customs broker.
  • Retreading's economics stand on their own. Transport Topics reports that fleets spend 30% to 40% less on retreads than on new tires on average. That gap does not depend on how this case ends. Our cost-per-mile breakdown covers the numbers.
  • Control over supply is the larger point. Trade cases, tariffs and price changes are outside a fleet's control. A fleet running its own retread line keeps more of its tire spend tied to its own casings and its own schedule. See casing ownership and our tariff guide.

What to do now

  • Watch November 30. CBSA's preliminary determinations will say whether dumping and subsidizing are occurring and at what margins. That is the first point at which a number could apply.
  • Ask suppliers about duty exposure. If you buy imported 17.5", 19.5", 22.5" or 24.5" truck, bus or trailer tires, new or retreaded, ask how provisional duties would affect your price and delivery.
  • Check scope with a customs broker. For imports near the edges of the product definition, CBSA's case documents and a licensed broker are the authority, not a blog post.
  • Review your casing program. Every casing a fleet retreads is one less finished tire it has to buy at whatever the market price turns out to be.
