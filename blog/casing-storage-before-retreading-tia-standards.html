Casing Storage Before Retreading: What TIA's Best-Practice Standards Actually Require

Ozone, UV, humidity, and stacking pressure can degrade a good casing before it ever reaches the buffer. What the industry's own storage guidance says — and why it becomes your problem once you run your own line.

Published September 18, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

A casing can pass every inspection point — no injuries, no separations, a clean NDT scan — and still come out of storage worse than it went in. Rubber is a living material even when it's just sitting on a rack, and the conditions it sits in between drop-off and buffing have a real, documented effect on whether it retreads well.

This isn't a niche concern. The Tire Industry Association (TIA) publishes its own best-management-practice guidance for tire storage specifically because the failure modes are well understood and preventable — they just require a shop or yard to actually follow them.

What actually degrades a stored casing

TIA's best-management-practices guidance, and the general warehousing literature that echoes it, converge on the same handful of threats:

  • Ozone. Electric motors, battery chargers, welding equipment, and generators all produce ozone as a byproduct — and ozone has a genuinely deteriorating effect on rubber. TIA's guidance is explicit: keep tire storage away from this kind of equipment, not just away from the weather.
  • UV and sunlight. Direct sunlight, and even light fixtures with a high UV index, dry out rubber compounds and cause cracking over time.
  • Heat and cold extremes. A cool, stable environment is the target — general guidance points to keeping storage roughly below 77°F and above freezing. Heat accelerates the same aging chemistry that ozone and UV drive; cold makes rubber stiff and more prone to cracking if handled roughly.
  • Humidity and ground contact. Moisture against a casing's steel belts and beads promotes mold and rust from the inside out — which is exactly the kind of internal damage a casing inspection is trying to catch before it's too late to matter.
  • Stacking and distortion. Casings left flat-stacked too long, or piled too high, can flat-spot or distort. TIA recommends storing tires upright on a pallet or rack rather than laid on the ground.

The practical rules

Factor TIA / general best practice
LocationIndoors, out of direct sunlight and away from ozone-generating equipment
TemperatureCool and stable — roughly below 77°F, above freezing
Ground contactOn a pallet or rack, never directly on the floor
OrientationUpright in racks preferred; limit stack height and rotate stock if stacked flat
Mounted casingsIf stored on rims, inflate to roughly 50% of normal operating pressure
Chemical exposureNo direct contact with solvents, fuels, lubricants, or disinfectants

Source: Tire Industry Association Best Management Practices for Proper Tire Storage, corroborated by general warehouse-racking industry guidance (Mecalux, Interlake Mecalux, AR Racking).

It's not just the incoming casings

The same rules apply on the other end of the line. A finished retread sitting in a warehouse before it ships back to a fleet is exposed to exactly the same ozone, UV, and humidity risks as the worn casing was before it was buffed — the new tread doesn't make the rubber immune to storage conditions. A shop that gets storage right on the intake side and sloppy on the outbound side is still taking on avoidable risk with finished product.

Why this becomes your problem when you run your own line

When a third-party retreader handles a fleet's casings, storage discipline — or the lack of it — happens on the retreader's property, out of sight. A fleet sending casings out never sees the rack, the temperature, or how long a batch sat before it was buffed.

Bring retreading in-house and that changes. Casings now sit in your yard between the time they come off a truck and the time they reach your buffer — and finished retreads sit in your warehouse before they go back out. Storage stops being a vendor's back-room process and becomes a line item in how you set up your own operation: rack space, a covered or indoor area away from your own electrical equipment and welding station, and a rotation discipline so nothing sits for months in a hot corner of the shop. None of this requires specialized equipment — mostly it requires knowing the rules exist and building them into how the space is laid out from day one.
