Powering an Electric Curing Chamber in Ontario: How Rate Plans and Cure Scheduling Shape the Bill

What an in-house retread shop pays per kWh in Ontario depends on its rate class and when the chamber runs. The November 2026 prices, the Class A/B line, and how to schedule cures around them.

Published November 21, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

With an electric curing chamber, electricity is the main energy input for curing. That's one reason many precure shops choose electric over steam: no boiler to run or staff. (We compare the two in Electric vs. Steam Curing Chambers.) But in Ontario, the price of each kWh isn't one fixed number. It depends on which pricing your account falls under, and for many small businesses, on what time of day the chamber is running.

This guide covers the parts of Ontario's electricity pricing that matter when a fleet brings retreading in-house. All prices are the ones in effect from November 1, 2026. We don't quote a "kWh per retread" figure: it varies with chamber size, load, insulation and how often the door opens. Get the chamber's rated electrical load from your supplier, then meter it in your own shop.

Step 1: Which pricing applies to your shop

Ontario business electricity pricing splits roughly by size.

  • Small businesses on the Regulated Price Plan (RPP). The IESO's Save on Energy guidance lists small businesses using 250,000 kWh a year or less as eligible for the regulated price plans set by the Ontario Energy Board (OEB). Distributor forms cite O. Reg. 95/05 for the exact eligibility bands, so confirm with your local utility where your account sits.
  • Larger accounts. Businesses above that level are served through other pricing options based on business type and average monthly peak demand. In practice, that means paying the market commodity price plus the Global Adjustment (GA). GA is a separate per-kWh or demand-based charge whose rates the IESO publishes.

For larger accounts, the next question is whether you're Class A or Class B for GA. Class A customers pay GA based on their share of a handful of province-wide peak hours, under the Industrial Conservation Initiative (ICI). Class B customers pay GA on every kWh. Per Hydro Ottawa's 2025 ICI guidance:

  • Facilities averaging 50–500 kW peak demand are not eligible for Class A.
  • Facilities between 500 kW and 1 MW can opt in only if their NAICS code starts with 31, 32 or 33 (manufacturing) or 1114.
  • Facilities between 1 and 5 MW can opt in. Those above 5 MW are Class A by default.

The IESO judges eligibility on average monthly peak demand over a base period that runs May 1 to April 30. A retread line on its own is unlikely to push a fleet maintenance facility past 500 kW. Most fleets adding an in-house line will therefore be on the RPP or Class B, and Class B pays GA on every kWh the chamber draws. Check your own demand history before you assume either way.

These are commodity prices only. Delivery, regulatory charges and HST come on top. The Ontario Electricity Rebate drops from 23.5% to 15.7% from November 1, 2026. Hydro One says it applies to small business customers and to any customer using under 250,000 kWh a year. The OEB says RPP prices are lower year over year, which is what allowed the rebate cut, so the net change on your bill depends on your usage pattern.

A small-business account using a meaningful amount of power quickly moves past the 750 kWh tiered threshold. For a shop running a curing chamber, the useful comparison is Time-of-Use against Ultra-Low Overnight.

Step 3: Why cure scheduling matters

A precure cure cycle lasts hours. Overdrive described one steam chamber curing 23 tires at a time over a curing period of about three hours. At one major retread system's training center, Fleet Equipment reported a chamber holding 22 tires per cycle. Curing took up most of the production time, and the full retread process ran about five to six hours. The same report noted the chamber heats up faster when a new batch goes into one that's already hot.

So a chamber cycle is a big, predictable block of energy use, and you can choose when it runs. The rate spreads in the table show why that matters. The figures below are simple arithmetic on the OEB prices, not a measured shop result:

  • On Ultra-Low Overnight, moving 100 kWh of chamber load from on-peak (37.7¢) to overnight (3.7¢) cuts its commodity cost from $37.70 to $3.70. Overnight power costs about one-tenth of on-peak.
  • On Time-of-Use, the same 100 kWh costs $18.90 on-peak and $9.10 off-peak. That's roughly half, and every weekend hour is off-peak.
  • The risk runs both ways. ULO's on-peak price is about double TOU's. A shop on ULO that cures during the weekday on-peak window pays much more than it would on TOU.

A schedule that fits these prices: prep, inspect, buff and build during the day, then load the chamber for an evening or overnight cure. Run back-to-back batches while the chamber is hot. Whether that works depends on your crew, your volume and your safety procedures.

Unattended curing is a safety decision first

A curing chamber is a pressurized vessel. Running it after the shift has gone home is not just a billing question. Before you schedule overnight cures, review your pressure-equipment obligations, lockout and monitoring procedures, and what your chamber's controls and alarms are designed for. We cover the Ontario rules in Workplace Safety for an In-House Retread Shop in Ontario. If the answer is "someone has to be present," price that labour into the schedule too.

For Class B accounts: the price signal is different

If you're a larger account paying market price plus GA as a Class B customer, the RPP time windows don't apply. The IESO publishes a Class B GA rate every month, and because Class B pays GA per kWh, every kWh the chamber uses carries it. Shifting load can still help when market prices are lower, but the size of the saving depends on your contract. Ask your distributor or retailer how your commodity and delivery charges are calculated. Delivery for larger accounts often depends on the rate class, not just total kWh.

What this means for the in-house decision

When you send casings to an outside retreader, energy is buried in the per-tire price. When you run your own line, it becomes a cost you can see and manage: by choosing the right rate plan, scheduling cures, and sizing the chamber to your volume so it runs full. That's one more operating lever a fleet keeps when it runs its own operation, and one more line in a realistic payback calculation.
