Rubber, Oil and Energy Costs in 2026: How Retreading Shrinks a Fleet's Tire-Price Exposure

Natural rubber was about 20% above year-earlier levels by April, and the World Bank expects energy prices to rise 32% in 2026. Why a retread carries less of that input-cost risk than a new tire, and what fleets can do about it.

Published November 17, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

Ask a fleet manager what drove tire costs in 2026 and you'll hear the same three words: rubber, oil, energy. They aren't just talking points. Tire Industry Association's Kevin Rohlwing, writing in FleetOwner this year, named those three as the factors truck tire prices depend on, and said natural rubber was trading near 10-year highs as supply tightened.

This post looks at what the current commodity data says, why a retread is less exposed to those inputs than a new tire, and what that means for a fleet deciding how much of its tire program to retread — or whether to run its own line.

What the 2026 commodity data actually shows

Two World Bank reports frame the year:

  • Natural rubber (April 2026 Commodity Markets Outlook). Natural rubber prices rose 5.2% in April 2026, after a 2% rise in the first quarter, leaving prices about 20% higher than a year earlier.
  • Energy (October 2026 Commodity Markets Outlook, as reported by Reuters on October 28). The World Bank now projects overall commodity prices to rise about 23% in 2026, with energy prices up 32%. It forecasts Brent crude to average $91 a barrel in 2026, up from $69 in 2025, before easing to $79 in 2027. The bank's baseline assumes conflict-related supply disruptions don't escalate further.

Synthetic rubber, carbon black and many process oils are petroleum-derived, and curing a tire takes a lot of heat. So when natural rubber, crude and energy rise in the same year, every tire on the market feels it. The question for a fleet is how much of that pressure lands on each tire it buys.

Why a retread carries less of the input-cost risk

The answer comes down to the casing. As FleetOwner's explainer on retreading puts it, a truck tire without its tread is called the casing, and it accounts for most of the cost, materials and energy required to build the tire. A retread reuses that casing. The new material is the tread rubber and cushion gum.

The Tire Retread & Repair Information Bureau (TRIB) puts a number on the oil side: the rubber components of a new medium truck tire take about 22 gallons of oil, while retreading the same tire takes about 7. For scale, Modern Tire Dealer's 2026 Top 50 U.S. Retreaders report measures retreaders by rubber used and estimates about 24 pounds of rubber per medium/heavy truck retread.

That doesn't make retreads immune. Tread rubber and cushion gum draw on the same natural rubber, synthetic rubber and carbon black markets, and FleetOwner's 2026 reporting on the U.S. retread industry describes retreaders dealing with rising costs alongside labour shortages and import competition. Rohlwing's own column expects retread prices to rise too. His point is that they rise by less in absolute dollars, because a retread costs about half as much as a quality new tire to begin with.

The exposure math, without invented numbers

You don't need a fleet case study to see how this works. Assume, as Rohlwing does, that a retread sells for about half the price of a comparable quality new tire. If input costs push both up by the same percentage, the dollar increase on the retread is about half the dollar increase on the new tire. Every position you move from new to retread cuts your exposure on that position by roughly half.

Fleet Equipment notes a second lever: when the fleet supplies its own casing and buys only the retread, the cost can fall to about a quarter of a new tire. The more of your own casings you put back into service, the smaller the share of your tire spend that tracks rubber and oil prices at all.

A few caveats keep this honest:

  • The ratios are trade-press estimates, not fixed rules. Actual retread-to-new price ratios vary by size, tread design, market and supplier. Run the numbers on your own quotes.
  • Cost per mile is what matters, not price per tire. A retread only saves money if the casing was managed well enough to retread and the retread runs its miles. Our cost-per-mile comparison and ATRI cost-per-mile breakdown cover that side.
  • Steer positions are usually out of scope. Fleet policy, and in some cases regulation, keeps most steer axles on new tires. See Retreads on the Steer Axle.

Practical moves for a Canadian fleet in a high-input-cost year

  • Protect the casing. The casing is where most of a tire's cost and energy sits, so every casing scrapped early is input cost you pay again. Inflation discipline and sensible pull points keep casings retreadable.
  • Review which positions run new rubber. Drive and trailer positions are where retreads are most widely used. Every position moved to retread roughly halves that position's exposure to the next round of price increases.
  • Know where your tires come from. 2026 has also brought trade measures that affect new-tire pricing in North America. We covered them in Section 232 tariffs on truck tires and Canada's truck tire dumping investigation. A fleet that reuses its own casings depends less on the import market.
  • Buy consumables on a plan, not in a panic. If you run your own line, tread rubber and cushion gum are the input-cost-sensitive part. A steady supply plan helps you avoid emergency buys. See consumables supply planning.

Where in-house retreading fits

When a fleet runs its own retread line, its input-cost exposure narrows to the tread rubber, cushion gum and curing energy for the casings it already owns. It decides when and how much to buy. That's a different position from absorbing whatever increase arrives on the next new-tire invoice.
