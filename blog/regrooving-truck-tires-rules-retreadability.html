Regrooving Truck Tires: The Rules, the “Regroovable” Marking, and What It Does to Your Casings

When a commercial tire can legally be regrooved, where a regrooved tire may run, what tire makers say about depth and timing, and how regrooving affects a casing you plan to retread.

Published November 21, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

Regrooving means cutting new grooves into the rubber left under a worn tread. It's an old technique in commercial tire programs, and it's still in use: a heated blade renews the pattern so the tire can run longer before it comes off for retreading. Done on the right tire at the right depth, it adds miles. Done on the wrong tire, too late, or carelessly, it's illegal in some cases and can cost you the casing.

For a fleet that runs, or plans to run, its own retread line, regrooving is really a casing decision. This guide covers what the regulations say, what tire makers recommend, and how regrooving fits a casing policy built around retreading.

What regrooving is, and isn't

Retreading puts new tread rubber on a buffed casing. Regrooving adds no rubber at all. It removes rubber from what's left of the original tread to deepen or re-cut the grooves. So it only works on tires built with enough rubber between the tread surface and the top belt, and that's why the rules start with the tire's design.

The US rules: 49 CFR Part 569

In the US, regrooving is governed by NHTSA's Part 569, "Regrooved Tires." The main points, as published in the CFR:

  • It covers your own tires too. Part 569 applies to all tires regrooved in the US. It applies equally to tires regrooved for sale and tires regrooved for the regroover's own use. A fleet regrooving its own tires is covered.
  • Only "regroovable" tires. The tire being regrooved must be a regroovable tire. A tire designed for regrooving must be labelled "Regroovable" on both sidewalls, molded on or into the rubber, with size and depth limits set for the lettering.
  • You can't add the marking yourself. NHTSA has said in an interpretation letter that a regroover can't add the marking before regrooving. It has to have been put there by the original manufacturer or a prior retreader.
  • Rubber must stay over the cords. After regrooving, the cord material below the grooves must be covered by at least 3/32 inch of tread material. New grooves must be between 3/16 and 5/16 inch wide.

The editions we checked were older CFR printings. The core requirements have been stable for decades, but check the current eCFR text before you write a regrooving procedure.

Where a regrooved tire can run

The FMCSA's equipment rule, 49 CFR 393.75, sets two position limits that matter here:

  • Buses: paragraph (d) says no bus may be operated with regrooved, recapped or retreaded tires on the front wheels. There's no load threshold.
  • Trucks and truck tractors: paragraph (e) says a regrooved tire with a load-carrying capacity of 2,232 kg (4,920 lb) or more may not be used on the front wheels of any truck or truck tractor. That captures typical heavy-truck steer tires.

In practice, that leaves regrooving for drive and trailer positions on heavy trucks. That's also where most fleets put retreads. See Retreads on the Steer Axle for how Canada's steer rules compare.

The Ontario rule

In Ontario, tire standards for vehicles on the road sit in Regulation 625 under the Highway Traffic Act. Published versions of Reg. 625 say a tire may not be regrooved or recut unless it was specifically designed for regrooving or recutting and is marked as so designed. The same regulation says a front tire on a bus must not have been altered by adding material to make a new tread surface. It also sets minimum tread depths: 3 mm for a front tire on a motor vehicle over 4,500 kg GVWR, and 1.5 mm generally.

The version of Reg. 625 we were able to review may not be the newest consolidation. Confirm the current text on Ontario e-Laws, and check with your provincial inspection authority if you operate in other provinces.

What tire makers say about timing and depth

Regulations set the floor. Tire makers' regrooving guides add the practical limits, and they don't all agree on the numbers:

  • Goodyear caps regrooving depth at 3 mm on its tires and wants 3 mm of undertread left under the new grooves. It suggests regrooving with 3–4 mm of tread left in regular highway use, or 5–6 mm where penetration damage is likely. If wear is irregular, it says to probe the remaining undertread first. It also says regrooving should be done only by fully trained operators using proven tools with electrically heated blades.
  • Matador's guidance allows regrooving down to 2 mm of residual undertread above the belt, but only on tires marked "Regroovable", and national rules still apply.
  • Hankook says the tire must be demounted before regrooving. It explains why minimum undertread matters: to avoid damage to the top steel belt, rib tearing from groove cracking, and stone damage.

The common thread: regroove early, while there's still rubber to work with, and follow the guide for the specific tire. Don't apply a generic depth across brands.

What regrooving does to a casing you plan to retread

This is where regrooving and retreading pull in different directions. Regrooving uses up rubber that protects the belts. Retreading depends on those belts being sound.

  • Thinner protection over the belts. Barum's regrooving guide warns that a thinner tread stock lets stones penetrate to the steel belts and cause rust, which it says has "a decidedly negative effect" on the tire's suitability for retreading.
  • Skill matters. UK trade coverage (Commercial Motor) warned decades ago that regrooving with about 2 mm of tread left can damage the breaker and lead to the casing being rejected if the work isn't done skilfully. The point still holds.
  • Cuts are damage the inspector has to find. A groove cut too deep or an exposed belt edge becomes a finding at casing inspection, and possibly a scrapped casing.

Michelin has argued, in trade press, that regrooving extends a tire's most fuel-efficient stage, because rolling resistance falls as tread wears. We couldn't find independent, published test data that puts a reliable number on that saving, so we don't quote one here. If fuel is your reason to regroove, ask the tire maker for its test data.

A regrooving policy for fleets that retread

If the casing is headed for your own retread line, its value as a casing usually outweighs the extra miles from a regroove. A sensible written policy might cover:

  • Eligibility: regroove only tires molded "Regroovable" on both sidewalls, and only in positions the rules allow (drive and trailer on heavy trucks; never bus front wheels).
  • Timing: regroove at or above the tire maker's recommended remaining depth, not at the legal minimum. The same logic applies as in setting pull points.
  • Method: demounted tire, trained operator, heated-blade tool, depth set to the maker's guide, undertread probed when wear is uneven.
  • Exclusions: skip regrooving on casings you've flagged as prime retread candidates, or on tires with cuts, stone drilling or irregular wear that already thins the undertread.
  • Records: log the regroove in the casing history, so your retread inspector knows the belt package has less rubber over it.
  • Measure it: compare casing salvage rate for regrooved and non-regrooved tires. If regrooved casings fail inspection more often, the extra miles may not be worth it.

Why this matters more on your own line

When an outside retreader rejects a casing, the fleet usually just sees a scrap tire or a smaller credit. When you run the line yourself, every rejected casing is direct cost. So is every casing that passes but fails early. That makes decisions upstream of the retread line, like when to pull a tire and whether to regroove it, part of your retread economics.
