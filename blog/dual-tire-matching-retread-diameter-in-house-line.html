Matching Duals: Why Buffed Diameter Control Matters on an In-House Retread Line

Mismatched duals scrub one tire and overload the other. What the published guidance says about diameter, pressure and tread depth, and why a fleet running its own buffer controls more of the variables.

Published October 19, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

Two tires bolted side by side on a drive axle are supposed to share the load equally. They only do that when they are the same height and the same pressure. When they are not, the smaller tire is dragged along the road surface while the larger one carries more than its share. Fleets that buy retreads from outside sources inherit whatever diameter the retreader delivers. A fleet running its own line has more say over it.

What the published guidance says

The figures below come from trade-press and industry material surfaced in our research. We could not open the primary documents (for example the Technology & Maintenance Council’s recommended practice itself), so the tolerances are reported as the trade press quotes them. Check the current TMC text before writing them into your own procedures.

  • Height tolerance. Fleet consultant Darry Stuart of DWS Fleet Services is quoted as saying TMC recommends keeping duals within about ¼ inch in height to limit scrubbing (Automotive Fleet / Trucking Info, “Match Dual Tires for Optimal Miles”).
  • How it is measured. FleetOwner’s “Dual matching nightmares” reports that the ¼-inch limit applies to tires standing side by side off the vehicle. On the vehicle, the technician measures radius, and the difference should be no more than ⅛ inch.
  • Pressure. Michelin advises that pressure between duals on the same axle should not differ by more than 5 psi, and a 5 psi spread is described as the accepted industry tolerance.

What a mismatch costs

A Tire Review column, “Matching Tires on All Axles,” gives load-shift figures. It reports that a 10% difference in air pressure means an 11% difference in the load each tire carries, that a ½-inch tread-depth difference adds about 2% load to the larger tire, and that a worn tire paired with a new one can add 55% load to the larger tire. The column is several years old, so treat the percentages as illustrative.

Other sources express the effect in pounds instead. The retread.org primer section on matching duals says a ¼-inch diameter difference may leave the larger tire carrying about 600 pounds more than the smaller. A Goodyear-sourced article reports that a 15 psi difference can put 500 pounds more on the higher-inflated tire. These figures use different baselines, so do not add them together or convert one into another. What they share is the direction of the effect: the bigger or harder tire works harder, the smaller one scrubs.

Why retreads need a closer look

FleetOwner quotes retreaders saying the diameter difference for the same size casing can be half an inch or more. Separately, trade press has noted that retreads can finish at the same outside diameter even from different casings, because modern computer-controlled buffers produce consistent diameters regardless of casing manufacturer. Put together, those two points suggest tread depth on its own is not a reliable matching check: diameter is the measurement that counts, and the buffing step is where it is set.

Where an in-house line has more control

This section is reasoning, not data. A tire’s finished diameter after retreading depends on the casing, the buffed profile and the tread rubber that goes on. A fleet running its own line decides the buffing target and checks it at its own station, then measures finished tires before they go back to service. A tire that leaves your shop is measured by your own people against your own limit.

  • Set a written diameter limit for dual positions, based on the current TMC practice, and record the revision you used.
  • Measure at two points: after buffing, and again on the finished tire before it is released.
  • Pair tires at the shop. Tag finished retreads with a measured height so the yard can match them at mounting.
  • Keep pressure in the program. Even perfectly matched diameters will not share load at mismatched pressures, which is why underinflation belongs in the same conversation.
  • Track returns. If scrubbed or uneven-wear tires keep coming back from certain positions, you have the data to find out why.

The buffing step itself is covered in our guides to buffing profile and crown radius and casing prep and bond quality, and our buffing machine page covers the equipment side. For the wider case for running your own line, see single-supplier lock-in versus running your own line and casing ownership.

What we are not claiming

  • We are not publishing a fleet example or a savings number for matching. The sources above give load-shift effects, not dollars.
  • A tolerance quoted in trade press is not a regulation. Your written limit should come from the current TMC practice and your tire and vehicle manufacturers.
  • Better matching alone does not make an in-house line pay. It is one of several quality controls the line brings under your roof.
