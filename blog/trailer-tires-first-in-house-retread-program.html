Start With the Trailers: The Lowest-Risk Way to Launch an In-House Retread Program

Why trailer tires are the natural first step for a fleet bringing retreading in-house — the regulatory, operational and economic case from published sources, plus a phased rollout plan.

Published November 16, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

A fleet weighing its own retread line usually pictures the whole thing at once: every size, every position, every casing in the yard. That's the hardest way to start. The trailer axles offer an easier first step. Retreads are already widely accepted there, the regulatory and liability questions are simplest, and the volume is large. And a trailer program builds the casing records and shop habits that a broader program needs later.

This guide sets out the case for a trailer-first rollout using published regulatory, EPA and trade-press sources. It doesn't use invented fleet examples. Your own tire records are what turn it into a number.

Why the trailer position is the natural starting point

1. It's the least demanding job a tire does

Trailer tires carry load, but they don't steer and they don't transmit drive torque. Trade press describes them as free-rolling, with less scrub from turning than tractor positions. That's why trailer treads are typically shallow rib designs (see our precured tread selection guide). For a new line, that means simpler tread specs and fewer combinations to stock while the crew learns the process.

2. It's already where fleets put retreads

Trade sources consistently describe the trailer as the main retread position. Heavy Duty Trucking reports that fleets tend to run a high share of retreads on trailers and typically run them down to the 2/32-inch federal minimum. HDT's Jim Park has also noted that plenty of fleets are content to cycle older drive and steer casings back to trailer positions "to let the trailer finish them off." Fleet Equipment describes the same cascade: drive tires come back as drive retreads, then go to trailer designs, and trailer tires come back as trailer treads.

A trailer-first in-house program isn't asking drivers, maintenance staff or customers to accept anything new. It moves work the fleet already buys outside into its own shop.

3. The rules are simplest there

US federal rules (49 CFR 393.75) set a 4/32-inch minimum tread depth for front-wheel tires on trucks and tractors, versus 2/32 for other positions. They bar retreaded tires on the front wheels of buses entirely. Canada's steer-axle rules are stricter still; see Retreads on the Steer Axle. None of those front-axle restrictions apply to trailer axles. A first-year program aimed at trailers leaves the hardest regulatory and liability questions for later. EPA's SmartWay program is also relevant here: it verifies retread technologies only for drive and trailer positions, so trailer retreads can still be part of a fuel-efficiency spec (more in our SmartWay retread guide).

4. Trailer tires are where many roadside problems start

The best-known figure is old, so treat it as directional. A Goodyear emergency-call-center analysis reported by FleetOwner in 2008 found that 65% of roadside service calls involved trailer tires, versus 22% drive and 13% steer. A FleetNet road-call sample reported by Heavy Duty Trucking (undated) found tire problems behind 48% of trailer road calls, and 53% for trucks and tractors. We couldn't find a more recent public breakdown, but the pattern is consistent: trailer tires get less attention than tractor tires, and they fail on the road more often.

An in-house program changes who handles those tires. When your own technicians inspect, repair and retread the casings coming off your trailers, they're the ones looking at underinflation damage, irregular wear and repair history. That matters for the casings you'll keep retreading. See underinflation and casing life and the 5-point casing check.

The economics: same retread math, narrower scope

The underlying savings are the same ones we cover in retreads vs. new tires. Transport Topics reports that fleets on average spend 30% to 40% less on retreads than on new tires. Retreading is also mainstream among large fleets: TRIB has said that 89% of fleets with 500 or more trucks use retreads (as reported in 2020).

What a trailer-first plan changes is the scale of the starting commitment, not the per-tire math:

  • Fewer sizes and tread specs. If your trailers run one or two standard sizes, a narrower range means fewer curing rims and envelopes and less tread inventory to start.
  • A smaller first line. A line sized for trailer volume costs less than one sized for the whole fleet. Our line-sizing guide and setup-cost guide show how throughput and equipment scale.
  • A cleaner payback test. Fewer variables make it easier to see whether your own cost per retread beats what you pay outside. The ROI guide walks through the inputs, and the ROI calculator runs them.

We're not putting a dollar figure on this. Payback depends on your trailer count, annual tire removals, casing salvage rate and what you pay outside today, and published sources can't supply those numbers. Pull them from your own records before you commit.

What trailer-first doesn't fix

Trailers aren't a free pass. Be honest about the limits:

  • High-scrub trailers are hard on treads. Spread-axle and tight-turning urban trailers need scrub-resistant compounds and solid shoulder ribs. Deep treads with decoupled shoulder ribs can tear in that service.
  • Casing quality still decides everything. Trailer tires that run underinflated or get dragged come back with damage no retread can fix. The salvage rate is the number to watch; see casing salvage rate as a KPI.
  • Retreading in-house still means running a manufacturing process. Inspection, buffing, building, enveloping and curing all have to be done right, with trained staff and proper records. See what FMVSS 117 requires and Ontario workplace-safety requirements.

Each phase builds on records from the one before, and each is a point where you can stop or scale. You decide that pace because you control the line. That's the structural advantage of running your own: an outside retreader, however good, sets its own capacity, tread range and pricing, and a fleet that depends on a single outside supplier has limited leverage over any of them.
