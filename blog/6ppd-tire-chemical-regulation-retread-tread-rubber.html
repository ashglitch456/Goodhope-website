6PPD in Tires: Where California and Canada Stand, and What It Means for Retread Tread Rubber

California regulates tires containing 6PPD, and its rule reaches new tread on retreads. Canada has 6PPD on its assessment list. What has happened, what hasn't, and what in-house retreaders should ask.

Published November 11, 2026 · Good Hope Retreaders

6PPD is a chemical added to tire rubber to protect it from ozone cracking. When it reacts with ozone it forms 6PPD-quinone, which washes off roads in stormwater and has been linked to deaths of coho salmon in urban streams. Regulators in the United States and Canada are now looking at it. For a fleet that retreads in-house, the question is practical: does any of this reach the tread rubber you buy? This guide sets out what we could confirm, what we could not, and what to ask your suppliers.

Sourcing note: we read the regulations, agency pages and trade reports through search-result extracts, not the full documents, and some sources disagree on dates. Where they do, we say so. This is a summary for planning, not legal advice.

California: tires containing 6PPD are a Priority Product

  • The listing. California's Department of Toxic Substances Control (DTSC) listed motor vehicle tires containing 6PPD as a Priority Product under its Safer Consumer Products program, effective October 1, 2023. It was the seventh product listed under those rules. Manufacturers selling such tires in California had to notify DTSC by November 30, 2023.
  • Retreads are partly in scope. DTSC's regulatory text says the definition does not include the used components of a retreaded tire, but the new tread material used in a retreaded tire is included. DTSC said the intent was to capture manufacturers of new tread material containing 6PPD. One law-firm summary describes retread materials more broadly as not covered; we have followed DTSC's own text.
  • Other exclusions. Tires sold into California as part of a new motor vehicle, and tires intended only for off-road equipment, are excluded.
  • Who must act. The obligations fall on manufacturers of the product, not on the shops or fleets that use it.

The search for alternatives is still running

Tire makers are meeting California's requirement through a consortium organized by the U.S. Tire Manufacturers Association (USTMA). As reported, it has 32 global tire manufacturers, including all USTMA members, and DTSC describes it as representing more than 90% of the California tire market. Its Stage 1 report identified seven potential alternatives to 6PPD for further study, and DTSC issued a notice of compliance that cleared the way for Stage 2.

The timeline is where sources differ. Earlier announcements gave August 19, 2026 as the Stage 2 due date. USTMA's current page and DTSC's product page give October 1, 2029 for the final analyses, with annual progress reports in between (the 2026 report was due October 1, 2026). We read that as the deadline having moved, but we could not find a document explaining the change. Either way, no replacement for 6PPD has been mandated yet.

Washington State

Washington passed legislation requiring its Department of Ecology to decide on initial regulatory actions for 6PPD in tires by June 1, 2025 and to adopt rules by June 1, 2026. We could not confirm whether those rules have been adopted, so check Ecology's current status before relying on it. Washington's bill used the same retread language as California's: used retread components excluded.

Canada: on the assessment list, no regulation yet

  • Data gathering. 6PPD was included in a federal mandatory data-gathering initiative published in June 2023.
  • Priority for assessment. After a public request under section 76 of the Canadian Environmental Protection Act (CEPA), the federal ministers responded on April 30, 2024 that 6PPD would be prioritized for assessment. The government's Plan of Priorities lists 6PPD and its transformation products, notably 6PPD-quinone, to be assessed together. The assessment will decide whether the substance is toxic or capable of becoming toxic under CEPA.
  • Status. We found no completed assessment and no proposed regulation as of this writing. A March 2026 environmental petition to the federal government asked what steps it is taking to regulate removing or replacing 6PPD in tires, which suggests the question is still open.

For an Ontario fleet, then, there is no Canadian rule on 6PPD in tread rubber today. What exists is a federal assessment under way, an active California program whose outcome tire and tread makers will likely apply across markets, and a direction of travel worth watching.

What it could mean for a fleet that retreads in-house

Our reasoning, not a published finding: tread rubber and cushion gum are rubber compounds, and an antiozonant is part of the recipe. If manufacturers change antiozonants to meet California's process, tread compounds sold elsewhere may change too, since compounders rarely run separate recipes for every market. A change in compound can mean changes to curing, storage or performance data. None of that is announced, and we are not predicting it. It is a reason to keep documentation in order.

  • Ask your tread rubber supplier the question. Ask whether the compound contains 6PPD and whether any reformulation is planned. Ask for the current technical and safety documentation for each product, and that applies to us too.
  • Keep records by batch. Note the tread and cushion gum product and batch on each retread. If a compound changes, you can then see what was used where. Our casing tracking guide covers the record-keeping side.
  • Re-check cure settings on any reformulated product. Follow the supplier's data for any new compound, and check storage limits (see tread rubber and cushion gum shelf life).
  • Watch the sources, not the headlines. The DTSC Safer Consumer Products page, USTMA's 6PPD page and the federal Plan of Priorities are where a real change will show first.

Retreading itself is not the issue here. The casing is reused, and DTSC's rule excludes the used components of a retread. The new tread is what regulators are watching. For context on retreading's environmental record, see our retread carbon footprint guide.
